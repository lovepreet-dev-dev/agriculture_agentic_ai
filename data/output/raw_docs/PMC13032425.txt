TITLE: Real-time identification and quantification of apple scab on fruit in preharvest and postharvest conditions using YOLO11: a deep learning approach
URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC13032425/

Apple scab (AS), caused by the fungal pathogen Venturia inaequalis , is a major disease of apple that manifests as lesions on leaves and fruits. The disease compromises fruit quality and yield, leading to substantial economic losses. Traditional AS assessment relies on visual scoring, which is labor-intensive, subjective, and poorly reproducible. This study proposes a deep learning-based framework to overcome these limitations and to enable an accurate, scalable AS phenotyping approach.
Deep learning techniques were employed for the object detection and segmentation of AS symptoms in apple fruits. A two-stage fine-tuning process was applied to color images collected under orchard and laboratory conditions using the YOLO foundation model (YOLO11). The model was first trained to detect healthy apple fruits (Model 1) and subsequently refined to segment AS lesions (Model 2) using high-resolution imagery (864 × 864 pixels). Model 1 (Fruit Detection) achieved 0.98 precision, 0.95 recall, and 0.94 mAP50. Model 2 (Lesion Segmentation) achieved 0.64 precision, 0.75 recall, and 0.75 mAP50. The framework supports real-time processing of images and video. Despite challenges such as variable lighting and symptom heterogeneity, the use of high-resolution training data improved the segmentation accuracy (mAP50-95) of fine-scale lesions by over 50% compared to the previous YOLO architecture.
These results demonstrate that the proposed deep learning-based approach provides a reliable pipeline for automated AS phenotyping. By improving precision and efficiency in both controlled and field environments, the model enhances apple grading assessments and accelerates breeding efforts to identify AS-resistant genotypes. Furthermore, this work establishes a solid foundation for broader applications in real-time plant disease monitoring and future integration of additional apple diseases.

Apple ( Malus domestica Borkh), a temperate fruit tree native to Central Asia, is one of the most widely cultivated and economically valuable fruit crops worldwide. Annual global production of apple exceeds 86 million tons, making the fruit one of the major components of horticultural systems and a key source of dietary nutrients and income for millions of growers [ 1 , 2 ]. Due to its sensory appeal as well as its nutritional and phytochemical richness providing dietary fiber, antioxidants, and polyphenols [ 3 , 4 ], apple is the second most consumed fruit globally after banana [ 5 ]. Its cultivation spans over 100 countries across diverse climates, owing to their extensive genotypic and phenotypic diversity [ 1 ]. This adaptability, as well as the broad production area, explains the large number of breeding initiatives aimed at improving resilience to biotic and abiotic stresses. Furthermore, breeding efforts increasingly focus on consumer-driven traits, such as flavor complexity, texture, and nutritional composition, reflecting the evolving demands of fresh and processed food markets [ 6 ]. The high value and widespread cultivation of apple make its protection from biotic stresses a major economic and research priority.
Concurrently with these breeding efforts, the expansion of organic farming reflects a growing emphasis on sustainability and environmentally conscious production. Organic agriculture is characterized by the avoidance of synthetic fertilizers and pesticides while promoting soil health and closed nutrient cycles [ 7 , 8 ]. These advantages have prompted growing consumer and policy interest in organically produced fruits, including apples. However, meeting the growing societal demand for the reduced use of synthetic pesticides makes apple production increasingly challenging, primarily due to susceptibility to biotic stresses [ 9 ]. The limitations of synthetic inputs in organic systems, combined with consumer demand for reduced pesticides, heightens the vulnerability of apple crops to diseases. Among these, apple scab (AS), caused by the ascomycete fungus Venturia inaequalis (Cooke) G. Winter, stands out as the most persistent and economically damaging disease limiting sustainable and organic apple production. AS severely compromises fruit quality by inducing lesions and cracking, leading to considerable economic losses, reduced yields, and an increased risk of mycotoxin contamination during storage [ 10 , 11 ].
In Switzerland, the humid and temperate climate favors the proliferation of V. inaequalis resulting in recurrent AS annual epidemics [ 10 , 12 ]. Organic orchards, there adopt alternative strategies for managing AS [ 13 ]. These approaches include cultural practices, such as pruning to improve air circulation, sanitation methods to reduce pathogen inoculum, and the application of approved organic fungicides, including sulfur-based products and plant-based extracts [ 14 , 15 ]. While measures can mitigate infections, the long-term solution lies in breeding cultivars with durable resistance to AS. Assessing this resistance, however, is a major bottleneck. Apple breeders have historically introduced genes derived from wild apples in new varieties that exhibit qualitative AS resistance. This qualitative resistance, controlled by a single gene, is easy to identify through molecular methods [ 16 – 18 ]. The most well-known example of qualitative resistance is Rvi6 (formerly Vf), widely used in apple breeding but increasingly overcome by virulent V. inaequalis races [ 21 , 22 ]. Other Rvi genes (e.g., Rvi1, Rvi2, Rvi4) also provide qualitative resistance, and breeders aim to “pyramid” multiple Rvi genes for improved durability [ 23 , 24 ]. Conversely, quantitative resistance, governed by multiple genes, is far more challenging, time-consuming and labor-intensive to evaluate accurately, underscoring the need for improved, efficient, and non-invasive diagnostic techniques that could enhance precision in disease management strategies.
Digital phenotyping using deep learning (DL) enables objective, large-scale disease quantification. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been successfully applied to automate the detection of AS and other fruit diseases, demonstrating significant accuracy and robustness in autonomously identifying disease-specific visual features with minimal human intervention [ 19 ]. For instance, Kodors et al. [ 20 ] achieved an accuracy of 85% using a VGG-16 model, while Patidar and Chakravorty [ 21 ], reported a lightweight CNN capable of 95% accuracy for classifying common apple diseases. Despite their performance, these models typically operate as image-level classifiers, lacking the ability to localize lesions or quantify symptom severity. Their inference times, often ranging from hundreds of milliseconds to over one second per image, depending on image resolution and hardware, which also limits their utility for real-time applications.
For real-time localization, the You Only Look Once (YOLO) family of models represents the state of the art [ 22 ]. YOLO models analyze the entire image in a single pass, drastically reducing detection time while preserving high accuracy. These characteristics make YOLO-based models highly suitable for in-field agricultural applications, including disease identification under variable lighting and background conditions [ 23 ]. Previous versions like YOLOv5 and YOLOv7 have demonstrated robust performance for AS detection on leaves and fruits under diverse environmental conditions [ 24 , 25 ]. However, such approaches typically emphasize binary classification, such as healthy versus unhealthy, without addressing the assessment of symptom severity, which is critical for symptom severity assessment.
The need for severity assessment has been explored through other CNN-based architectures. For example, Rath [ 26 ] fine-tuned a ResNet34 model, achieving a validation accuracy of 94% on a dataset comprising images of healthy and scab-infected apples. Similarly, Kavya et al. [ 27 ] compared multiple CNN architectures (including VGG-16, ResNet-50, and MobileNet). The MobileNet model achieved the highest accuracy at 98% in detecting and quantifying AS severity. Despite their promising results in both studies, the image-based detection methods are limited to controlled environments or to datasets with uniform lighting conditions, which restricts their robustness under varied or adverse environmental conditions commonly encountered in orchard settings.
This gap in robust, in-field severity assessment is not fully addressed by existing commercial solutions. Modern optical systems still depend on costly hardware solutions rather than lightweight AI models [ 28 ]. For instance, sorting machines using multi-spectral sensors can accurately classify apples based on surface disorders, including AS lesions. However, their high-cost limits accessibility, especially for smaller or organic producers. These systems, moreover, are typically restricted to providing binary accept/reject decisions based on fixed thresholds. This critical limitation prevents the calculation of a single disease severity assessment, thereby ruling out nuanced applications such as monitoring disease progression during storage, providing decision support for postharvest treatments (e.g., hot water treatments) or identifying early sale windows for some apple batches. Consequently, improving AS management requires advancements at two key stages: preharvest and postharvest. Preharvest improvements involve enhancing the selection of resistant cultivars by improving the early detection and accurate classification of AS symptoms, thus benefiting breeding programs and targeted disease management strategies. Postharvest advancements focus on developing methods to monitor symptom progression during storage for the early detection of scab progression and refining sorting processes to accurately assess AS severity, an operation that cannot be achieved with a sorting machine [ 29 ]. Although minor scab lesions are accepted to some extent in organic apple production, particularly for apples sold as table fruit in Switzerland [ 30 ], this limited tolerance underscores the importance of accurate severity-based grading (i.e., assigning a numerical level that reflects symptom advancement and extent) and the need for reliable assessment methods to ensure proper assessment and marketability.
To address these challenges and limitations, this study presents a lightweight DL framework for real-time, cost-effective disease assessment across both orchard and laboratory conditions. For this purpose, it was leveraging the segmentation variant of the newest iteration of the YOLO model, YOLO11-seg, selected for its efficient architecture and YOLO11 optimized segmentation head (the architectural component responsible for generating pixel-wise masks, streamlined to improve accuracy and reduce computational cost) that generated precise, pixel-wise masks with lower computation cost. The model was trained on a comprehensive image dataset containing healthy and scab-infected apples, enhanced through data augmentation techniques. This approach offers a robust, efficient, nondestructive tool for use in real-time evaluation, using either images or video in both orchard and postharvest settings. This approach aims to offer a cost-effective and precise alternative that can significantly improve disease monitoring and apple grading accuracy, contributing meaningfully to more sustainable and precise disease management practices.
Images were captured using a Sony RX1-RII camera equipped with a Sonnar lens of 20–35 mm f/2.0 and a 42.4 megapixels full-frame sensor. The camera was powered by a 16 V battery bank, connected to a step-down voltage transformer to provide the required 5 V operating voltage. A portable, adjustable overhead camera rig was constructed using a Walimex telescopic background support system (1.2–3.0 m height). For images under laboratory settings, apple samples were manually placed randomly in baskets (H: 13 cm x W: 40 cm x L: 60 cm). The camera was mounted on the horizontal crossbar positioned to maintain a consistent nadir (downward looking) orientation at a height of approximately 1.75 m above the ground (accounting for the table height 0.75 m) and roughly 1 m from the basket and uniformly illuminated by daylight fluorescent bulbs (85–5500 K).
For the orchard imaging, a single telescopic pole was utilized as a monopod with the camera at 1 m height and 1 m from the target fruit. Images were captured from multiple angles (45˚, 60˚, and 90˚ relative to the target) to ensure a diversity of perspectives during mornings (9:00 to 11:00) and afternoons (14:00 to 18:00) hours varying weather conditions, including both cloudy and sunny days, to ensure robustness. All images were taken only on trees that were first evaluated by phytopathologists identifying the AS score and the different apple disorders and stored in JPEG format with a resolution of 3984 × 2656 pixels. Camera settings were manually optimized for fruit and AS symptoms clarity, ISO at 100, shutter speed 1/60 and 1/125, F-stop f/8 and manual focus. A multispectral camera was initially evaluated but proved to be no more informative than the RGB one. An overview of the imaging setup of both imaging setups is presented in Fig. 1 .

The image dataset collection consisted of apple samples exhibiting natural and high- severity AS infections collected from two organic production sites in Switzerland: from the Research Institute of Organic Agriculture (FiBL, Frick; elevation 350 m), with images captured under both real field and postharvest conditions. And the Swiss Confederation’s center for agricultural research (Agroscope, Conthey; elevation 504 m) of fruits under storage conditions originated from various nearby organic farms. The fruits were stored under normal atmosphere conditions at 0–4 °C and with a relative humidity of 90–95%. The timing of symptom appearance on fruits strongly depends on the infection period and the prevailing environmental conditions, beginning on spring and continuing throughout the growing season. During this period, daily minimum and maximum temperatures ranged from 12 °C to 22 °C ± 2 °C, with relative humidity varying between 65% and 70% (agrometeo.ch). Prior to imaging, apples were taken out of the storage and left at ambient temperature for approximately 1 h to minimize surface condensation and humidity-related artifacts that could compromise image quality and analysis. Apple images were captured at multiple, variable time points throughout the storage trial to monitor symptom progression until the apples were completely rotten. The time points varied across the different cultivars; some samples were taken twice, while others three or four times. This approach enabled the documentation of dynamic changes in visual appearance due to storage-related physiological or pathological developments, supporting both early detection efforts and severity assessments during postharvest evaluations. In addition, for the robustness of the model, several publicly available labeled and unlabeled datasets of apples with and without AS, samples with other non-identified lesions (other diseases or mechanical damages) from Roboflow [ 31 ], and images provided by Kodors et al. [ 20 ] and uploaded in the Kaggle database were included (supplementary file Table 1 ). An example of the variability of the dataset is shown in Fig. 2 .

AS phenotyping and visual scoring are traditionally performed at multiple stages, including in the orchard, immediately after harvest, and during storage. In orchard conditions, visual assessments are conducted by trained evaluators (mainly phytopathologists) who inspect a representative sample of fruits per tree using standardized scales [ 33 ] (Table 1 ). Postharvest phenotyping is carried out in controlled indoor environments, with each fruit manually rotated to ensure complete surface examination. Based on the presence, number, and size of the lesions, the fruits are then sorted into different categories like fresh market/storage, processing/juice, and reject/waste. Only fruits in optimal condition and free from defects are selected for storage. Fruits with minor lesions, affecting 1–25% of the surface area and no indication of significant decay are allocated for juice processing. Apples with more extensive lesions are either designated for animal feed or discarded. During storage trials, fruits are periodically inspected to assess overall health and monitor disease progression. Each fruit is evaluated for lesion size, number, and distribution, with visual differentiation performed to distinguish AS lesions from other storage-related disorders, such as lenticel breakdown or fruit rots.

To mitigate illumination bias caused by variable lighting, the “Gray World” algorithm [ 34 ] was applied to normalize color balance across the entire dataset. This algorithm assumes the average scene reflectance is achromatic and normalizes RGB channels accordingly.
The annotation protocol was performed in a two-stage process by two independent annotators. First, macro-level objects (individual apples) were segmented and labeled as “apple” using the “Anylabeling” software [ 35 ], which incorporates artificial intelligence (AI) models, such as the Segment Anything Model (SAM) [ 36 ]. Second, micro-level objects (disease symptoms or defects) were meticulously annotated using LabelMe software. These were categorized as “scab” and “disease” for other disorders (e.g., bitter pit, rots). To handle ambiguity, temporary labels (e.g., scab2, rotten_apple) were initially used and later consolidated by a postharvest pathology expert to ensure biological accuracy.
To assess annotation quality control, the inter-annotator agreement (IAA) was carried out on a random subset of 50 images (approximately 0.5% of the total dataset) and was independently annotated by both annotators. The average Intersection over Union (IoU) for segmentation masks was 0.83, indicating high consistency and an acceptable score given the complexity of the task, particularly due to the closeness of the annotations to the borders of the lesions. A separate check by a professional expert in postharvest management, quality analyses and fruit physiology and pathology, was conducted to verify the biological accuracy of the class labels. All annotations, originally generated in JSON format for LabelMe were converted into YOLO format using the “labelme2yolo” tool to meet the input requirements of the YOLO segmentation model.
To enable the model to specifically learn the distinct symptoms of AS, a subset of images with severe infections (covering more than 80% of the fruit surface) was selected. Using the OpenCV package (Open-Source Computer Vision Library) [ 37 ], scab-affected regions were zoomed and cropped to generate new images consisting exclusively of scab. Subsequently, all images in the complete dataset were verified to ensure all objects were annotated and correctly labeled. The systematic error of duplicate annotations, which occurred in approximately 40% of cases, was also addressed. These duplicates were removed to ensure each apple, disease or defect was represented by a single annotation.
Finally, the raw dataset consisted of 10,276 images, which was expanded to 42,000 images through augmentation techniques with the OpenCV library, Transformations included horizontal and/or vertical flipping, translation, zooming, and rotation (Table 2 ; Supplementary File S1).
At the instance-level counts, the dataset exhibited a substantial class imbalance: “apple” instances were the most represented (103661 instances; 59.6%), followed by “scab” (51773; 29.7%) and “disease” (non-scab symptoms) (18573; 10.7%), for a total of 174,007 annotated instances. This imbalance reflects the natural structure of the images: apples occupy larger regions of the frame, whereas scab lesions and other symptoms are smaller and more fragmented, inherently making minority-class detection more challenging.

The models were trained on a Lenovo ThinkPad P15 computer with an Intel Core i9 processor, an NVIDIA Quadro RTX 5000 Max-Q GPU (16 GB), and the Ubuntu 22.04 64-bit operating system.
To ensure the optimal performance of the object detection and segmentation models, several preliminary tests and iterative refinements were conducted before finalizing the training pipeline. Initially, YOLOv8 was selected due to its strong balance between speed and accuracy in real-time segmentation tasks. At the time, it was one of the most widely supported models for segmentation. With the release of YOLO11, the model architecture was updated due to its enhanced support for custom anchor generation, an important feature for detecting small and irregularly shaped objects, such as scab lesions. YOLO11 divides the input image into a grid of S × S cells, following the same approach as previous YOLO versions. Each cell is responsible for detecting objects whose centers fall within their boundaries. This grid-based strategy enables the model to predict bounding boxes and class probabilities in a single forward pass, thereby maintaining real-time detection capabilities. YOLO11 directly predicts the center coordinates and dimensions of objects relative to each grid cell, which simplifies the detection process and reduces computational complexity, resulting in more efficient and accurate object detection [ 38 ]. In this study, the default YOLO11 detection head was employed without any manual anchor tuning. Unlike earlier YOLO versions that relied on predefined anchor boxes, YOLO11 adopts an anchor-free prediction strategy, making anchor values not applicable in this context. An overview of this process is illustrated in Fig. 3 . In addition, Version 11 has an improved backbone, allowing detailed spatial features needed for segmentation, maintaining accuracy, and reducing computational time. The primary convolutional (Conv) block from previous versions is retained but is connected to the Cross Stage Partial with kernel size 2 (C3K2) after each iteration. The C3k2 block enhances the overall performance of the feature aggregation process, making it faster and more efficient. Additionally, the spatial pyramid pooling - fast (SPPF) block is connected to the cross-stage partial with spatial attention (C2PSA) block. C2PSA improves spatial attention in feature maps, allowing the model to focus more effectively on specific areas of interest within the image. To ensure a rigorous evaluation of the model’s generalization capability and prevent data leakage, a stratified group-based splitting protocol was employed to partition the dataset into training, validation, and test subsets. As images were captured of the same individual fruit under different conditions (e.g., varying angles, lighting, and backgrounds) and augmentations derived from a single original source image were assigned to the same group. The groups were then divided based on their class label, either healthy (images of apples in perfect conditions) and unhealthy (images of apples with scab, other diseases and/or defects) to maintain a consistent distribution. The groups were randomly mixed and allocated to the training, validation, and test sets. This guaranteed that the model is evaluated on completely novel fruit instances that it could not have seen in any form during training.
The annotation strategy underwent significant evolution. In the initial approach, apples were labeled as either “healthy” or “unhealthy” with additional polygon annotations on unhealthy apples to denote specific disorders such as scab, general disease, or unknown defects. However, this method introduced interpretability issues, as YOLO treats each class as an independent instance and lacks the capability to associate an apple with its internal disease regions. Consequently, early models trained using this labeling method struggled to distinguish between healthy and unhealthy apples reliably, often causing confusion during inference. To address this, a two-step training approach was first attempted: (1) detecting apples and classifying them as healthy or unhealthy, and (2) fine-tuning the model to localize disease regions only on detected unhealthy apples. This yielded suboptimal results, prompting a re-evaluation of the labeling strategy. The revised approach unified all apples under a single “apple” class, while disease regions (e.g., scab, other diseases, and unknown disorders) were treated as separate classes. A key enhancement included label prioritization, where overlapping annotations were reordered to give precedence to scab regions, which is critical for the accurate segmentation of small lesions.
Despite this improvement, the initial experiments still showed low precision, particularly for scab detection. The datasets were then split based on environmental conditions (e.g., indoor vs. outdoor images), but false negatives persisted, particularly among the “unknown”, “disease” and “background” classes. The “unknown” labels were removed due to ground-truth insufficiency, and all non-scab pathologies were grouped under a general “disease” label. This adjustment significantly improved precision, although performance remained below expectations.
To further enhance generalization, a staged training approach was adopted. The model was first trained on publicly available datasets with well-annotated disease labels to establish a strong baseline. It was then fine-tuned using the custom dataset collected in this study. However, this strategy also yielded results comparable to earlier tests, suggesting that data origin was not the primary limitation affecting performance. The final training strategy employed a robust two-phase approach for YOLO11-seg architecture. Extensive hyperparameter tuning, including adjustments to batch size, learning rate, and training duration, was performed using the Ultralytics tuner, identifying the optimal values through weighted random mutations.
In the first phase, the dataset consisted solely of images of healthy apples (6050 images: 5550 from public databases and 100 from orchard/laboratory sources); split into 70% training (4235 images), 15% validation (908 images), and 15% testing (907 images). The model was trained for 100 epochs using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) optimizer with a momentum of 0.9 and an initial learning rate (lr0) of 0.001 to adapt the pretrained backbone to healthy apple morphology. Convergence was deemed sufficient due to the low appearance variability of the single object class. Furthermore, an early stopping mechanism based on a patience parameter of 10 epochs was employed to halt training if validation loss showed no significant improvement, thereby mitigating the risk of overfitting. This initial phase was intended to learn general object structure, shape variability and segmentation boundaries.
In the second phase, fine-tuning was performed using a reduced learning rate to preserve the representations learned in phase one. This phase utilized a heterogenous dataset of 3192 raw images, containing healthy apples, scab infected apples, and apples with other non-scab defects, split according to an 80/10/10 scheme (2554 training, 319 validations, and 319 testing images). This was augmented by merging a dataset of 1659 images created from cropped scab-lesion (also split 80/10/10 into the corresponding partitions. Thereafter, data augmentation was applied, ensuring preservation of class distribution within each subset. For this phase, the AdamW optimizer was employed with a lower learning rate of lr0 = 0.0005 to refine representation of scab and other disease lesion while preserving previous learned features. The training duration was set to 300 epochs; this larger number was necessary by greater heterogeneity and a significantly more imbalanced class distribution, of the mixed dataset. Early stopping was automatic, ensuring that the model reaches a stable optimization of lesion-level features found at 250 epochs, consistent with findings from ablation experiments indicating that minority-class recall increased primarily during late training. For both phases, the parameter that defines the number of images processed in one training iteration (batch size) was set to 8. In this way, it was balanced training efficiency with memory requirements and stable updates [ 39 ]. Additionally, extra augmentations, specifically variations of the HSV (hue, saturation, and value) color image representation were applied to enhance model generalizability and reduce overfitting. To evaluate the model’s performance, several standard metrics were used. Precision (P) assesses the model’s ability to avoid false positives (Eq. 1), while recall (R) measures its effectiveness in detecting all relevant instances of a class (Eq. 2). The F1-Score measures classification accuracy, which is especially used in unbalanced datasets (Eq. 3). Average precision (AP) combines both precision and recall into a single score (Eq. 4). Mean average precision (mAP) computed at various intersection over union (IoU) thresholds provides a comprehensive evaluation of localization accuracy (Eq. 5). Typically, an IoU threshold of 0.5 is used to determine whether an object is correctly detected (acceptable performance), while a range of thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 in 0.05 increments offers a more nuanced assessment of the model’s performance.

Prior to the final label consolidation, an initial model was trained with a more granular set of annotations aimed at distinguishing specific disease and defect classes (e.g., lenticel rot, bitter pit, spot anthracnose, and other rots). The performance of this model, as summarized in the normalized confusion matrix (Supplementary Figure S1 ), revealed several critical patterns. While predictions for most classes aligned along the diagonal, significant misclassifications occurred. For instance, ‘healthy_apple’ was incorrectly predicted as ‘unhealthy_apple’, and ‘scab’ was confused with ‘healthy_apple’ and ‘background’.
Furthermore, the high degree of similarity in physical symptoms across several disease categories presented a considerable challenge, as the visual characteristics were not distinct enough for fine-grained classification. This limitation was clearly reflected in the Precision-Recall curves (Supplementary Figure S2), which showed critically low Average Precision (AP) for most specific disease classes (e.g., ‘lenticel_rot’, ‘rotten_apple’). The curves indicated that any attempt to increase recall for these classes resulted in a drastic reduction in precision, confirming the model’s inability to reliably distinguish between them. This performance gap was likely exacerbated by a severe class imbalance, with the dataset containing significantly more ‘scab’ (AS lesion) samples than other diseases. Therefore, to improve the model’s overall performance, all apple instances were consolidated into a single ‘apple’ class. The ‘disease’ class was defined to encompass all other pathological disorders and significant defects that are visually distinct from scab. This includes, but is not limited to, bitter pit, lenticel rot, other fungal rots, and clear mechanical damage. The decision to consolidate these into a single class was driven by two primary factors. First, the initial model struggled with fine-grained classification among these less-frequent diseases due to a severe class imbalance and high visual similarity between some symptoms. Second, for the practical application of this model sorting apples based on overall fitness for market the critical distinction is often between ‘scab’ and ‘other defects’ rather than identifying the specific type of non-scab defect. The ambiguous ‘unknown’ label was removed to eliminate a source of confusion for the model, as training on uncertain labels can significantly degrade performance. This consolidation strategy prioritizes robust and reliable detection of the presence of any defect over the more error-prone task of identifying its specific type, which aligns with the core objectives of this study.
The final model was configured with three classes: ‘apple’, ‘scab’, ‘disease’. A distinct class-size imbalance was evident in the per-class instance counts, which were split into COCO size bins (small < 32² px², medium 32²–96² px², large ≥ 96² px²). Apple instances were overwhelmingly large, whereas scab lesions were predominantly medium-sized. The ‘disease’ annotations were fewer overall but were distributed across all size bins (Fig. 6 ).
The comparatively lower performance on the ‘scab’ class is consistent with its predominance of medium and small instances. Detection models generally achieve higher accuracy on large, well-defined objects, while medium and small instances (where scab dominates) are more challenging to detect accurately. These object-size histograms strongly indicate that this imbalance is a significant contributing factor to the observed performance gaps.

To confirm the robustness of the selected two-phase configuration and validate the impact of key hyperparameters, a series of ablations studies was conducted. The results of these experiments, summarized in Table 5 , demonstrate the critical importance of a structured training strategy.
The one-phase baseline (combining healthy, scab and disease/defects apple samples) training on the full dataset failed catastrophically, achieving near-zero mAP scores. This complete model collapse confirmed that the complexity and class imbalance of the full dataset are prohibitive for standard end-to-end training. Similarly, reversing the intended training order, by first training on AS infected and other disease/defects samples dataset before fine-tuning on healthy apples yielded suboptimal results. Although this scheme achieved high Box and Mask mAP50 scores (0.884 and 0.843, respectively), its significantly lower mAP50-95 scores (0.617 and 0.522) indicated a model with high precision but poor recall, which struggled to generalize across IoU thresholds.
In contrast, the proposed model configuration training on ‘healthy’ and fine-tuning in AS infected and other disease/defects samples proved highly effective. When trained for a period of 150 epochs, this configuration achieved strong performance, with a Mask mAP50 of 0.861 and a Mask mAP50-95 of 0.683. Further investigation showed that extending the training period to 250 epochs led to even better results. This extended training achieved near-perfect performance for all classes (~ 0.99 Box mAP50, ~ 0.94 Mask mAP50) and the highest overall robustness (~ 0.95 Box mAP50-95, ~ 0.85 Mask mAP50-95), demonstrating stable convergence and superior generalization. Additional ablation studies were also tested, by reducing the batch size from 8 to 4, which led to a slight decrease in all metrics but maintained stable convergence, indicating that the method is not overly sensitive to this hyperparameter. Similarly, removing HSV color space augmentations from the pipeline, it resulted in a noticeable drop in recall and specifically hindered scab performance. This underscores the value of this augmentation technique for improving model generalization and its ability to learn critical disease features. Normalized confusion matrices for each ablation can be found in the supplementary file (Figures S3 to S7) (Table 3 ).

The performance of the initial model, trained exclusively on images of healthy apples, demonstrated strong performance, achieving a mAP@50 of 97%. This confirmed its ability to accurately identify apples under indoor and outdoor conditions. Additionally, the model obtained mAP@50–95 for “all” (healthy apple fruit) classes of 0.94 for Box and 0.92 for mask, indicating that the predicted bounding boxes and masks were very precisely aligned with the ground truth, as shown by the metric results in Table 4 . The final model, trained on images captured under indoor postharvest and outdoor in orchards conditions, including apples with scab symptoms and other disorders, achieved a mAP@50 of 89% for all the classes, specifically 75% for AS, confirming its capability to effectively identify healthy from unhealthy apples, and scab like-lesions from other defects (Table 5 ). A critical analysis of the per-class results reveals a significant trend: while the quantity of data is important, the inherent complexity and visual characteristics of the target class are paramount determinants of performance. This is best illustrated by comparing the ‘apple’ and ‘scab’ classes. The ‘apple’ class, characterized by its large size, uniform color, and distinct shape, achieved near-perfect detection and segmentation (mask mAP50: 0.96, mAP50-95: 0.93). The model’s high recall (0.98) for this class confirms its exceptional ability to locate nearly all whole apples in an image.
Conversely, the ‘scab’ class, despite being represented by the largest number of instances (12,366), proved to be the most challenging, yielding the lowest performance metrics (e.g., mask mAP50-95: 0.68). This result underscores a key challenge in fine-grained plant phenotyping. The scab’s small size, irregular morphology, and visual similarity to other surface blemishes make precise localization and segmentation inherently difficult. While the results showed a moderate mask recall (0.75) and precision (0.64) indicating that the model’s predictions for scab are often correct, it could also fail to detect portions of scab lesions. This can be attributed to the varying outdoor conditions, where shadows, changes in light, and other undesired objects (leaves, branches, etc.) can interfere with detection. The ‘disease’ class, which likely represents larger, more generalized symptomatic areas, performs robustly (mask mAP50: 0.94), further supporting the conclusion that object size and visual distinctiveness are critical factors in performance.
To evaluate the specific benefit and efficiency of the YOLO11 model, the YOLOv8m-seg was trained and tested on the same dataset using an input size of 864 × 864 pixels and executed on the same workstation (RTX 5000 GPU). Table 6 shows the results obtained with YOLOv8 after fine-tuning process. According to the results YOLOv8 has slightly lower computational cost (110 GFLOPs) in comparison to YOLO11 (123 GFLOPs) but it uses more parameters (27.2 M) and a bigger on-disk size (61 MB vs. 68 MB), indicating a less compact and parameter-efficient design. Conversely, Table 5 shows that YOLO11 yielded higher overall recall and F1-score, particularly for the scab class. For instance, scab lesions, YOLO11 were better segmented with a recall from 0.60 to 0.78 and mAP50-95 from 0.44 to 0.70, reflecting that this version has a stronger ability to detect small, irregular lesions. Although inference time increased from 29.4 ms to 38.0 ms (32 vs. 26 FPS), it uses fewer parameters (22.3 M) and a smaller on-disk size (61 MB), indicating a more compact and parameter-efficient design. The results confirm that YOLO11-seg provides a better balance between sensitivity and speed, making it more suitable for this application of deployment in orchard monitoring and sorting-line environments.

To assess the statistical robustness of the model’s performance, 95% confidence intervals (CI) were computed using a bootstrap procedure (2000 resamples) applied to the last 20 stable training epochs. For bounding-box predictions, the model achieved a precision of 0.921 (CI 0.920–0.922), recall of 0.843 (CI 0.843–0.844), mAP50 of 0.896 (CI 0.896–0.896), and mAP50–95 of 0.749 (CI 0.749–0.750). For mask-based segmentation, precision was 0.876 (CI 0.875–0.876), recall 0.811 (CI 0.811–0.811), mAP50 0.860 (CI 0.860–0.861), and mAP50–95 0.678 (CI 0.678–0.678). The extremely narrow confidence intervals across all metrics indicate negligible epoch-to-epoch variability and confirm that the model reached a stable convergence regime. These results support the robustness and reproducibility of the reported performance values and demonstrate that both detection and segmentation heads exhibit statistically robust behavior during the final phase of training (Fig. 9 ).

The practical applicability of the proposed model was assessed by measuring its end-to-end latency on an RTX 5000 GPU. The system achieved a latency measured in milliseconds (ms) of 21.7 ms at 640 pixels, 37.4 ms at 864 pixels and 53.6 ms at 1024 pixels. The corresponding number of complete images, or frames, that a system can process or render per second (FPS) was also calculated using Eq. 7, providing a result for the same image sizes (640, 864 and 1024 pixels) of 46.1, 26.7, and 18.7 FPS respectively. The model’s high FPS confirms its suitability for real-time applications, such as evaluations in the orchards or sorting lines in laboratory conditions.
To evaluate the model’s performance on power-efficient edge devices, its throughput was projected for the NVIDIA Jetson platform. This estimation, summarized in Table 7 , reveals the critical trade-offs in deploying a high-performance model on a resource-constrained device. Using the Jetson Xavier NX as an example, performance in precision was estimated at approximately 40% of the desktop throughput, while INT8 precision provided an additional 1.5x speedup over FP16.
Based on these projections, the model is estimated to achieve an end-to-end throughput of 18.5/10.8/7.5 FPS in FP16 and ~ 27.4/16.0/11.2 FPS in INT8 for the respective image sizes. Assuming a one-apple-per-frame scenario, this translates to a processing capacity of up to 1,110-1,644 apples per minute (apm) in a real-world sorting system, which was calculated using Eq. 8.

The YOLO-based detection and segmentation model was then implemented in a pipeline to analyze scab disease severity. These preliminary results can be observed in Fig. 10 , with each image displaying the model’s outputs, including object detection bounding boxes, segmentation masks, class labels (e.g. “apple”), and associated confidence scores. The detection process involved extracting bounding boxes for each detected object, assigning a unique Apple ID, and calculating the proportion of diseased area relative to the total apple area. Disease severity was quantified using the scoring system, where severity scores ranged from 1 (healthy) to 9 (highly diseased) based on proportion affected percentage [ 33 ]. The results were automatically stored in an Excel file containing details such as Apple ID, classification, total apple area, disease-affected area, and computed disease percentage. Additionally, an output image was produced, displaying the Apple ID with the bounding box and its corresponding segmentation for clear and interpretable visualization (Fig. 11 ). The proposed approach enhances the efficiency of automated fruit quality assessment while maintaining high interpretability and computational efficiency, making it suitable for real-time agricultural applications.

To validate the model’s effectiveness, its performance was tested against human expertise using a randomly selected dataset of 100 images containing both scab-infected and healthy fruit. Although the algorithm provides a score for each apple instance, these scores were averaged per image to enable a direct comparison with expert ratings, which were assigned at the image level. The detailed results are presented in Supplementary Table 2.
The agreement between the proposed automated grading and the expert ratings was high (Table 8 ), as evidenced by strong linear and monotonic associations. The mean bias was 0.12, indicating that the expert’s average score was only approximately 0.1 points higher than the model. In addition, a Bland–Altman analysis was performed to further assess the agreement. The limits of agreement (−0.90 to 1.14) show that 95% of paired scores differed by less than ± 1 severity grade. The agreement was tight, with only a single high-severity outlier (mean ≈ 5.6, difference + 3.0) that suggests an occasional divergence at the upper severity levels, Fig. 12 . This outlier was attributed to the model’s ability to detect even partial apples, which experts excluded from the area of analysis. The mean absolute error was 0.22, and the root mean square error (RMSE) was 0.53. These metrics, along with an 80% exact agreement rate, confirm that the system can reliably grade scab severity across the 0–9 scale (Table 1 ).
In addition, the system achieved a processing latency of 38.9 ms per image (0.3 ms preprocessing, 38.0 ms inference, 0.6 ms post-processing), corresponding to 26 FPS or about 1,560 apples min⁻¹. Manual evaluation of scab severity typically requires several seconds per fruit, depending on disease severity and evaluator expertise [ 40 ]. This comparison demonstrates that the automated model not only matches expert-level grading accuracy but also performs orders of magnitude faster, enabling large-scale, objective, and reproducible assessments that are impractical for human evaluators.

For a practical field-deployment scenario, we evaluated the model’s inference speed on hardware representative of both high-performance and embedded systems. Using the final selected weights, the model achieved an average inference time processing of approximately 0.6 s (≈ 1.7 frames per second) when processing a high-resolution image (1616 × 1080 pixels). This performance demonstrates the model’s viability for offline analysis tasks, such as the automated grading of apple batches on the primary training GPU (NVIDIA Quadro RTX 5000). To simulate a more accessible field-deployment setting, the model was projected on a lower-power, embedded system, Jetson Xavier NX. On this hardware, the projection using same image resolution showed that the model maintained a viable average inference time of 85 ms per image (approximately 12 frames per second), demonstrating its suitability for real time applications.
This study developed and validated a two-stage YOLO11-based instance segmentation framework for the detection and severity assessment of AS on fruit surfaces under both controlled and orchard conditions. The proposed methodology combines high-resolution RGB imaging with a staged fine-tuning strategy, enabling accurate lesion localization, robust segmentation, and quantitative severity estimation in a single forward pass. This approach directly addresses key limitations of conventional visual scoring, which is subjective, labor-intensive, and poorly scalable, particularly for quantitative resistance phenotyping and postharvest quality assessment [ 41 ].
The two-phase training strategy proved essential for achieving stable convergence and high lesion sensitivity. Pretraining the network on healthy fruit allowed the model to learn generic apple morphology, surface texture, and illumination patterns, thereby establishing a robust feature representation that was subsequently refined for disease-specific cues during fine-tuning on scab-infected samples. This staged optimization mitigated the strong class and size imbalance inherent to the dataset, in which large apple instances dominate while scab lesions occupy comparatively small and heterogeneous regions. The resulting improvement in recall for the scab class, together with the reduction of false positives at lesion margins, demonstrates that hierarchical feature adaptation is critical for small and irregular target segmentation especially in agricultural imagery.
Compared with classical two-stage segmentation frameworks such as Mask R-CNN, which have been successfully applied to fruit disease localization [ 42 ] but require region proposal generation and incur higher computational overhead [ 43 , 44 ]. In addition, the YOLO11-seg architecture offers a more efficient end-to-end solution than R-CNN. Foundation models such as SAM and SAM2 exhibit remarkable generalization capabilities [ 36 , 45 ], yet their fine-tuning and inference costs remain challenging for real-time deployment and high-throughput phenotyping. In contrast, YOLO11 provides fully automated instance segmentation with low latency and a compact computational footprint, making it particularly suitable for inline inspection and field robotics. The anchor-free detection head and decoupled classification–regression branches of YOLO11 further enhance robustness to scale variability, which is a defining characteristic of scab lesions that range from a few pixels to extended necrotic patches.
Several studies in apple disease analysis have explored YOLO-based architectures, for example, the AppleScab model integrating CARAFE into YOLOv7 reported a mAP(0.5–0.95) of 64% at an input resolution of 416 × 416 pixels [ 46 ], demonstrating real-time capability but limited spatial precision for fine-grained lesion delineation. Other works employing YOLOv5 or YOLOv8 variants for apple or citrus disease detection achieved high classification accuracy but were restricted to bounding-box localization and did not provide instance-level severity quantification [ 20 , 21 ]. More recent studies using YOLOv10-based detectors have addressed occluded fruit detection by incorporating finer-scale feature layers and attention mechanisms to improve sensitivity to small and low-contrast targets in complex orchard scenes [ 47 ]. While this study establishes YOLO11 as a robust baseline for apple scab phenotyping, the object detection landscape has evolved rapidly since the model’s release, introducing critical optimizations for agricultural deployment that define the future roadmap for this research. YOLOv12, released in early 2025, integrated Large Language Model (LLM)-generated synthetic data pipelines to enhance feature generalization, with recent studies demonstrating its superior precision over YOLO11 in commercial apple orchards by effectively expanding training diversity [ 48 ]. Following this, YOLOv13 (mid-2025) introduced hypergraph-enhanced attention mechanisms to capture high-order spatial correlations, significantly improving small-object detection in cluttered canopies. Most recently, Ultralytics released YOLO26 (January 2026) [ 49 ], marking a paradigm shift towards end-to-end, NMS-free (Non-Maximum Suppression) inference. Unlike YOLO11, which relies on heuristic NMS post-processing that can suppress closely clustered fruit, YOLO26 utilizes a streamlined architecture optimized for edge deployment, removing the Distribution Focal Loss (DFL) module to accelerate inference. Migrating the segmentation protocols developed in this study to the YOLO26 architecture represents a clear path for future optimization, potentially reducing latency on edge-compute platforms while eliminating the hyperparameter tuning required for NMS in dense fruit clusters.
However, despite the raw performance gains of YOLOv12, v13, and YOLO26 [ 50 ], existing literature on these models largely focuses on bounding-box detection or yield estimation. There remains a significant gap in instance-level segmentation for quantitative disease severity assessment. This study addresses that gap, by validating a two-stage fine-tuning protocol on the stable YOLO11-seg architecture, providing a methodological framework that is backbone-agnostic. The contribution is therefore not merely the adoption of a specific detector version, but the integration of a training strategy that stabilizes feature learning on healthy fruit morphology before refining lesion-specific representations.
Although, several methodological limitations remain such as extremely small or early-stage scab lesions, occupying only a few pixels, can be confused with background noise or surface artifacts, particularly under strong shadows or non-uniform illumination. Partial occlusion by leaves, branches, or overlapping fruits may lead to incomplete masks and underestimation of disease severity. This design constitutes a methodological advance for automated phenotyping that can be readily adapted to newer backbones like YOLO26 in future work.
Beyond the YOLO family, the emergence of Real-Time Detection Transformers, such as Roboflow’s RF-DETR [ 31 , 51 ], presents a compelling alternative for complex orchard environments. Recent benchmarking on green-fruit detection indicates that RF-DETR can outperform both YOLO11 and YOLOv12 in scenarios characterized by severe occlusion and “green-on-green” camouflage [ 52 ]. The transformer architecture’s global attention mechanism allows RF-DETR to better model context compared to the local receptive fields of YOLO models, potentially reducing false negatives where scab lesions are partially obscured by leaves. However, this accuracy currently comes at the cost of higher computational memory and latency requirements compared to the lightweight YOLO11-seg used in this study. Future hybrid pipelines could prove advantageous, for example, by combining RF-DETR for high-precision fruit detection in complex canopies with a lightweight YOLO-based segmentation head for rapid lesion delineation [ 52 ].
A big constraint, either with more advanced or future models, is the current 2D imaging configuration, which captures only one side of the fruit at a time, potentially resulting in false negatives when lesions are located on the hidden surface. These constraints highlight the potential benefits of multi-view acquisition, controlled illumination, geometric normalization, and temporal integration for progressive symptom tracking that can be as well explore in future experiments.
Overall, the proposed YOLO11-based pipeline here presented demonstrates that real-time instance segmentation and objective severity quantification of apple scab on fruit are feasible under both laboratory and field conditions. By bridging high-throughput computer vision with plant pathology and postharvest quality control. This work provides a scalable methodological foundation for precision phenotyping and automated disease monitoring that is well-aligned with ongoing developments in next-generation architectures.
This study validated the YOLO11-seg model for detecting and quantifying AS disease severity on apple fruits under both orchard and laboratory conditions. A two-phase training strategy, combined with a diverse multi-source dataset, enabled the model to generalize effectively across heterogeneous imaging environments.
The final model (model 2) achieved strong performance metrics, 0.89 precision, 0.84 recall, 0.89 mask mAP50, and a mask mAP50–95 of 0.79. These results confirm its ability to reliably distinguish scab lesions from other surface defects. Comparative evaluation revealed high agreement with expert ratings (MAE = 0.22; RMSE = 0.53), proving the method reduces subjectivity and ensures reproducibility in horticultural quality control. By standardizing scab detection, this work contributes to precision horticulture and breeding programs focused on sustainable and disease-resilient apple production.
However, limitations remain. The detection of very small or early-stage lesions is challenging due to spatial resolution and class imbalance. Furthermore, heterogeneous orchard illumination reduces contrast, compromising segmentation, while the current 2D acquisition setup inevitably misses lesions on the unobserved side of the fruit.
To ensure robust field deployment, future work should incorporate multi-view imaging (360° assessments) to eliminate blind spots. In addition, next-generation architectures must be explored, specifically the untested YOLO26, to enhance the model’s capacity to detect small, irregular scab lesions and improve computational efficiency in complex field settings.
Deployment on edge-computing hardware, such as the NVIDIA Jetson series, also represents a key avenue for enabling real-time, in-orchard disease monitoring. Expanding the framework to support multiple apple disorders and developing an integrated multi-disease phenotyping platform would further broaden its applicability.
