TITLE: Comprehensive virome analysis of potato crops in Bangladesh reveals diverse viral communities and the first report of potato virus V
URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC13126838/

Potato crops in Bangladesh are significantly threatened by viral diseases, underscoring the need for comprehensive virome profiling to inform sustainable management strategies. In this study, we employed high-throughput sequencing (HTS) to characterize the viral diversity in symptomatic potato leaves collected from five major potato-growing districts. A total of 10 distinct viruses were identified, representing six families and nine genera. Among these viruses, potato virus X (PVX) was the most abundant, followed by potato virus Y (PVY) and potato virus S (PVS). Notably, potato virus V (PVV) was detected for the first time in Bangladesh and, to our knowledge, not previously reported in South Asia. Additionally, we assembled the complete genome sequence of a potato virus A (PVA) isolate from Bangladesh, which was highly similar to previously reported PVA genomes. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the Bangladeshi PVA and PVV isolates share close evolutionary relationships with global strains, particularly those from Europe and the Americas, suggesting historical or trade-related linkages. Co-infection analysis showed a high frequency of mixed infections, with PVY, PVS, and potato leafroll virus (PLRV) being the most common combinations. The correlation analysis identified strong statistical associations among specific viruses [e.g., between potato aucuba mosaic virus (PAMV) and tomato leaf curl New Delhi virus (ToLCNDV)], indicating frequent co-occurrence patterns that warrant further investigation; however, causal mechanisms were not inferred from these data. Geospatial analysis revealed that Dinajpur and Panchagarh districts had the highest overall viral prevalence, while Thakurgaon exhibited the lowest diversity of infections. These findings highlight the complexity of the potato virome in Bangladesh, emphasizing the threat posed by the dominance of PVY and the emergence of novel viruses like PVV. This study provides a foundational framework for future research on potato virus epidemiology and paves the way for sustainable agricultural practices to mitigate the impact of viral infections on potato production in Bangladesh.

Potato ( Solanum tuberosum L.) is the world’s third most important food crop, serving as a staple for millions and contributing significantly to global food security and agricultural economies. In Bangladesh, potatoes are the most widely cultivated food crop after rice and wheat, playing a vital role in rural livelihoods and the national agricultural economy. Favorable agroecological conditions have enabled Bangladesh to become the fourth-largest potato producer in Asia and the seventh-largest globally, with an annual production exceeding 10 million metric tons [ 1 – 3 ]. However, this substantial production is increasingly threatened by viral diseases, which often result in severe yield losses.
Viral diseases pose a serious threat to potato production worldwide, with yield losses often exceeding 50% in infected crops [ 4 ]. These losses are exacerbated by rising temperatures, which boost populations of insect vectors responsible for virus transmission, and by the lack of virus-free seed systems in many potato-growing regions. Globally, more than 50 viruses are known to infect potatoes, with some causing yield reductions of up to 80% in cases of mixed infection [ 5 ]. In Bangladesh, viral infections typically lead to yield losses of 20–50%; in severely infected fields or under poor management, losses can reach 80–90% [ 6 , 7 ]. Such losses are primarily attributed to viruses like potato virus Y (PVY), potato leafroll virus (PLRV), and others that are widespread in the region and pose persistent threats to potato cultivation.
While notable potato viruses such as PLRV, PVY, and potato virus X (PVX) have been documented in Bangladesh, knowledge of the full spectrum of viruses affecting potato crops—and their combined impact on productivity—remains limited. Earlier studies using RT-PCR detected eight viruses in potatoes across three locations [ 8 ]. However, those studies could not identify novel viruses or assess the prevalence of mixed infections. Emerging viruses like tomato leaf curl New Delhi virus (ToLCNDV) and tomato chlorosis virus (ToCV) add further complexity to potato virology in South Asia. Both viruses, primarily transmitted by the whitefly Bemisia tabaci , have been reported in neighboring countries and could affect potatoes due to their broad host range and rapid regional spread [ 9 – 11 ]. Their emergence in Bangladesh poses a growing biosecurity threat, highlighting the need for advanced detection and management strategies. Moreover, Bangladesh’s tropical climate and the abundance of insect vectors such as aphids and whiteflies create ideal conditions for virus transmission. Addressing these challenges requires a better understanding of the potato virome to mitigate economic and production impacts.
Traditional diagnostic methods (e.g., PCR and ELISA) have been critical for identifying individual potato viruses, but they are limited in detecting mixed infections and novel viruses. High-throughput sequencing (HTS) offers a transformative approach to plant virology, enabling comprehensive and unbiased virome analysis [ 12 ]. HTS can detect both known and previously unreported viruses, and it can characterize complex viral communities that might go unnoticed with conventional methods [ 13 ]. This technology facilitates a deeper understanding of viral diversity and interactions, providing critical insights for disease management [ 14 – 17 ]. Given the limitations of prior studies in Bangladesh, adopting HTS allows a far more detailed exploration of the potato virome, filling critical knowledge gaps and improving virus management strategies.
Viral infections not only reduce potato yields but also degrade tuber quality, directly affecting farmer livelihoods and the agricultural economy of Bangladesh. The lack of virus-resistant potato varieties and limited vector control measures further aggravate the problem. Comprehensive virome studies can guide the development of sustainable agricultural practices by enabling early diagnosis, informing the use of virus-free planting materials, and improving integrated pest management strategies. These efforts will bolster potato production and contribute to food security in Bangladesh and beyond.
In this study, we used HTS to investigate the virome of potato crops in five major production zones of Bangladesh: Rangpur, Dinajpur, Panchagarh, Thakurgaon, and Gazipur. The primary objectives were to identify previously unreported viruses, evaluate the prevalence and diversity of mixed infections, and gain insights into the viral community dynamics within potato crops. Despite the importance of potato cultivation in Bangladesh, the potato virome remains understudied. To address this gap, our study presents the first complete genome sequence of potato virus A (PVA) from Bangladesh and documents the first occurrence of potato virus V (PVV) in Bangladesh and South Asia. We also highlight the diversity and prevalence of potato-infecting viruses, including emerging threats such as ToLCNDV and ToCV. These findings underscore the pressing need for enhanced surveillance and management strategies to mitigate the impact of viral pathogens. By uncovering the full virome of potato crops, this work aims to improve food security and strengthen biosecurity measures in Bangladesh and other potato-growing regions of South Asia, where viral diseases continue to threaten agricultural productivity and livelihoods.
The geographic distribution of sampling sites and representative symptomatic potato plants are shown in Fig. 1 . Four key locations in the northern part of Bangladesh and one in the central part (Gazipur) were selected for sampling between December 2022 and January 2023 (Fig. 1 A). These regions were chosen due to their importance as intensive potato cultivation zones and the frequent occurrence of virus-like symptoms in potato fields (Supplementary Figure S8). The selected locations represent agroecologically diverse areas, ensuring comprehensive coverage of potential disease hotspots. Potato leaf samples displaying characteristic viral disease symptoms were collected from fields in these areas. Representative symptoms included severe mosaic patterns, leaf deformation and rolling, chlorosis, vein clearing, and curling (Fig. 1 B). Such symptoms suggested the presence of single or mixed viral infections and therefore warranted further investigation using high-throughput sequencing. From each location, ten symptomatic leaves were randomly selected from different fields (total n = 50) and processed for further analysis.

Total RNA was extracted from each of the individual potato leaf samples using the NucleoSpin RNA Plant kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. After extraction and quality assessment, equal amounts of RNA from all 50 individual samples were pooled to generate a single composite RNA sample for high-throughput sequencing. The quality and quantity of the extracted RNA were evaluated using a NanoPhotometer NP80 (IMPLEN, Munich, Germany) and a 4200 TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The pooled RNA samples were stored at -80 °C until library preparation. High-throughput sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Macrogen, Seoul, Korea).
RNA-seq data were processed using the CLC Genomics Workbench (version 22.0.1, QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) (Supplementary Figure S1). Low-quality reads and adapter sequences were removed using the “Trim Reads” tool. The host genome sequence of Solanum tuberosum (GenBank assembly GCF_000226075.1) was obtained from the NCBI; trimmed reads were aligned to the potato reference genome, and unmapped reads were extracted to remove host-derived sequences from the dataset. De novo assembly of the remaining reads was then performed in CLC Genomics Workbench to construct contigs (word size: 23, bubble size: 50). The resulting contigs were analyzed by both BLASTn and tBLASTx. For sensitive detection of viral sequences, tBLASTx searches were first conducted against the viral RefSeq database (NCBI) to enrich for virus-derived contigs. Only contigs with E-values less than 1e-5 were considered putative viral hits and were subsequently subjected to BLASTn searches against the NCBI nucleotide database for species identification. Virus classification and naming followed the guidelines of the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) [ 18 ].
To determine virus prevalence across individual samples and regions, all viruses identified by HTS were subsequently validated using targeted RT-PCR or PCR assays. Primers were designed based on the assembled viral contigs using the NCBI Primer-BLAST tool (See Supplementary Table S1 for primer sequences). RT-PCR amplification was performed using the SuPrimeScript RT-PCR Premix (GENETBIO, Daejeon, Korea), a one-step RT-PCR kit. Reverse transcription and amplification were carried out under the following thermal conditions: 50 °C for 30 min, initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 58–60 °C for 30 s (depending on target), and 72 °C for 1 min; with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. For DNA viruses, conventional PCR was performed with an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 58–59 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min; and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were separated by agarose gel electrophoresis, and target bands were purified using the Expin SV Gel and PCR Clean-Up kit (GeneAll, Seoul, Korea) for Sanger sequencing when necessary.
To assess intra-species genetic variation, trimmed sequencing reads were mapped to the assembled viral reference genomes. SAM alignment files generated during the mapping process were extracted and visually inspected using Tablet software to confirm read alignment patterns and validate polymorphic sites across the viral genomes. Base count information at each genomic position was extracted from the mapping results in tabular format. For each nucleotide position, the counts of A, C, G, and T were used to calculate total read depth. The major allele count was defined as the highest nucleotide count among A, C, G, and T at a given position. Positions with zero total read depth were excluded from frequency calculations. Minor allele frequency (MAF) was calculated for each genomic site, and sites with a minor allele frequency ≥ 5% were defined as significant polymorphic positions. The total number of significant SNVs was determined for each viral genome to assess the population-level genetic diversity within the pooled samples. For visualization, variant allele frequency (VAF) plots were generated using Python (pandas and matplotlib libraries). Genomic position was plotted on the x-axis and minor allele frequency (%) on the y-axis. All genomic positions were displayed in gray, and significant polymorphic sites (≥ 5% MAF) were highlighted in red.
Full-length consensus sequences of each identified virus were aligned with representative global isolates retrieved from NCBI. Alignments were created using the CLC Genomics Workbench alignment tool. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the maximum likelihood method in MEGA11. Branch support was evaluated with bootstrap analysis using 1,000 replicates [ 19 , 20 ]. Bootstrap values are provided to indicate the confidence of branching patterns in the phylogenetic trees. Trees were rooted and out grouped according to virus-specific criteria (e.g., using a distantly related virus as an outgroup) and visualized to assess the evolutionary relationships of Bangladeshi virus isolates relative to global sequences.
Presence/absence infection matrices were converted into binary numerical values and analyzed using Pearson correlation to quantify pairwise associations among viral species. Correlation matrices were generated in Python (pandas v1.5.3) and transformed into an edge list table. Network analysis and visualization were performed in Cytoscape (v3.10.4). Edges were weighted by the absolute correlation coefficient and colored using a continuous diverging scheme (blue–white–red) corresponding to positive, neutral, and negative associations, respectively. Node size and label settings were standardized across the network. Force-directed layouts were applied with subsequent manual spatial refinement.
Using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, a total of 62,608,204 raw reads were generated from the pooled potato leaf samples. After quality trimming and adapter removal, 99.9% of reads were retained. Alignment to the potato genome (host subtraction) reduced the dataset to 46.7% of the original reads, and subsequent filtering to retain viral sequences left 38.1% of the reads for de novo assembly (Supplementary Figure S1B). The non-host reads were assembled into 1,786 contigs, of which 270 were identified as viral in origin.
Following assembly, we remapped the cleaned reads to each viral consensus sequence to quantify viral abundance (Fig. 2 ). Virus read counts were normalized by genome length and expressed as reads per kilobase to allow relative comparison among viruses. Among the identified viruses, PVX exhibited the highest normalized read abundance (673.7 reads/kb), followed by PVY (608.4 reads/kb). Potato virus S (PVS) and potato virus M (PVM) yielded 508.3 and 450.8, respectively. In addition, 437.3 corresponded to potato aucuba mosaic virus (PAMV), and 231.6 to PLRV. A lower abundance was associated with four additional viruses: potato virus A (PVA), potato virus V (PVV), tomato chlorosis virus (ToCV), and tomato leaf curl New Delhi virus (ToLCNDV) (Fig. 2 ). Specifically, PVA contributed 59.0, PVV contributed 46.0, and ToLCNDV and ToCV accounted for 0.7 and 0.3, respectively (Fig. 2 ). In our dataset, PVX accounted for the largest share of normalized viral reads followed by PVY and PVS, indicating that these viruses were present at high abundance in the pooled potato samples. PLRV and viruses with lower read counts PVA, PVV, ToCV, ToLCNDV were detected at comparatively lower abundance. Overall, the read mapping results showed the dominance of PVX and PVY in the sequenced potato virome, and revealed the complexity of multiple co-infecting viruses present in Bangladeshi potato crops.

Bioinformatic analysis of the symptomatic potato leaf samples (representative symptoms shown in Supplementary Figure S8) revealed the presence of multiple viruses spanning diverse taxonomic groups. Table 1 summarizes the detected viruses, including their families and genera, ICTV-approved species names, consensus genome lengths, GenBank accession number, and prior reports of their occurrence in Bangladesh. In total, 10 distinct viruses were identified, comprising nine RNA viruses and one DNA virus (the begomovirus ToLCNDV). These viruses belong to the families Potyviridae , Betaflexiviridae , Alphaflexiviridae , Solemoviridae , Closteroviridae , and Geminiviridae .

Among the RNA viruses, two members of Potyviridae —potato virus Y (PVY) and potato virus A (PVA)—were identified with consensus genome lengths of 9,673 nucleotides and 9,553 nucleotides, respectively. Potato virus X (PVX, 6,449 nt) and potato aucuba mosaic virus (PAMV, 7,037 nt), both from Alphaflexiviridae , as well as PLRV (5,882 nt) from the family Solemoviridae , were also detected. All of these viruses (PVY, PVA, PVX, PAMV, PLRV) have previously been reported in Bangladesh (Table 1 ). Notably, although PVA has previously been reported in Bangladesh, this study generated the first complete genome sequence of a PVA isolate from potato in the country.
Notably, potato virus V (PVV), another Potyviridae member with a consensus genome length of 9,857 nt, was identified for the first time in Bangladesh in this study. To our knowledge, PVV has not previously been reported from Bangladesh, and its detection here represents the first molecular evidence of its presence in the country. PVV has been reported mainly from Europe and South America, and its occurrence in Bangladeshi potato samples expands the known geographic distribution of this virus. PVV was detected for the first time in the potato virome of Bangladesh.
In addition, we detected two viruses that are more commonly associated with other solanaceous crops: ToCV and ToLCNDV. ToCV, a bipartite crinivirus belonging to the family Closteroviridae , was identified by the presence of both RNA1 (8,565 nt) and RNA2 (8,185 nt) genome segments. ToLCNDV, a bipartite begomovirus in the family Geminiviridae , was identified by the presence of its DNA-A (2,740 nt) and DNA-B (2,684 nt) segments (Table 1 ).
Our virome analysis also confirmed the presence of two calravirus species from Betaflexiviridae : PVM (8,501 nt) and PVS (8,485 nt). Both PVM and PVS were found in the samples, indicating the presence of multiple viruses in the studied fields.
To investigate the genetic diversity and population structure of the identified viruses across the surveyed regions, trimmed reads were mapped to the 12 assembled viral genomic components representing 10 virus species , and single nucleotide variants (SNVs) were subsequently analyzed. The trimmed reads were continuously mapped across the entire viral genomes without detectable gaps (Fig. 3 ), indicating high coverage and assembly reliability. Based on variant allele frequency (VAF) analysis, numerous significant SNVs (≥ 5% minor allele frequency) were identified in each viral genome (Supplementary Figure S9). SNV frequencies were normalized to genome length and expressed as the number of significant SNVs per kilobase (Table 2 ). The genome-length–adjusted SNV frequencies were 86.9 SNVs/kb for tomato leaf curl New Delhi virus (ToLCNDV) DNA-A and 106.2 SNVs/kb for toLCNDV DNA-B. For tomato chlorosis virus (ToCV), the normalized values were 19.4 SNVs/kb for RNA1 and 13.8 SNVs/kb for RNA2. Among the potato-infecting viruses, the SNV frequencies were 20.0 SNVs/kb for potato virus A (PVA), 95.1 SNVs/kb for potato virus Y (PVY), 118.0 SNVs/kb for potato virus X (PVX), 65.9 SNVs/kb for potato virus V (PVV), 90.5 SNVs/kb for potato virus S (PVS), 222.2 SNVs/kb for potato virus M (PVM), 46.1 SNVs/kb for potato leafroll virus (PLRV), and 89.8 SNVs/kb for potato aucuba mosaic virus (PAMV). Across all analyzed viral genomes, including bipartite and segmented genomes, normalized SNVs were distributed throughout the full-length genomic regions rather than being concentrated in specific loci.

We used RT-PCR and PCR assays to confirm the presence and assess the distribution of ten viruses detected by HTS in potato samples collected from five districts of Bangladesh (see Supplementary Table S2 for representative gel results). All confirmed virus sequences were deposited in GenBank under accession numbers PX317643–PX317654 (Table 1 ). Our analysis revealed significant regional variation in virus prevalence and co-infection patterns:
Rangpur : Seven different viruses were detected in Rangpur. Notably, ToCV and ToLCNDV were consistently found in all samples (100%), indicating that these two viruses are well established in this district. PVS, PAMV, and PVM also showed very high prevalence (each detected in 100% of samples), while PLRV was found in 75% and PVY in 62.5% of samples. In contrast, PVA, PVV, and PVX were not detected in any of the Rangpur samples (Supplementary Tables S2, S3). Dinajpur : Dinajpur exhibited one of the highest levels of virus diversity, with all ten viruses detected and frequent co-infections observed. Several viruses—including PAMV, PVM, PVY, ToCV, and ToLCNDV—were present in all samples (100%). The remaining viruses (PVS, PVA, PVV, PLRV, PVX) were also common, being detected in approximately 55–89% of samples (PVS 88.9%; PVA, PVV, PLRV, and PVX each around 55.5%) (Supplementary Table S3). Mixed infections involving multiple viruses were widespread in Dinajpur. Panchagarh : This district also showed a high rate of co-infection, with all ten viruses detected. Most of the viruses, including PVY, PVX, PVS, PVA, PAMV, PVV, ToCV, PLRV, PVM, and ToLCNDV, were found in 40–100% of samples. For example, PVY, PVX, PVA, PAMV, PVV, and ToLCNDV were detected in all samples (100%), ToCV and PVM in 60%, and PVS and PLRV in 40% of samples. Extensive viral co-occurrence was observed in Panchagarh (Supplementary Tables S2, S3). Thakurgaon : Thakurgaon had the lowest virus diversity among the northern districts, with six viruses detected. PVY and PVV were the most prevalent, each detected in all samples (100%). ToLCNDV and PLRV were found at moderate frequencies (62.5% and 37.5%, respectively), while PVS and PVA were detected in 25% of samples. PAMV, PVM, ToCV, and PVX were not detected in samples from Thakurgaon. Gazipur : Gazipur (the only central-region site in our study) showed the presence of eight different viruses. PVS and PVY were the most frequently detected, both present in all samples (100%). PVV was also highly prevalent (87.5%), while PLRV, PVA, and PAMV were detected at moderate levels (37.5%, 25%, and 25%, respectively). PVX and ToLCNDV were found at lower frequencies (12.5% and 25%, respectively). ToCV and PVM were not detected in Gazipur, indicating a regional difference in their distribution compared with the northern districts (Supplementary Table S2). Across all regions, PVY was the most widely detected virus, occurring at 100% frequency in four districts and at a prevalence of 62.5% in Rangpur. In contrast, ToCV was detected only in the northern districts (Panchagarh, Rangpur, Dinajpur) and was not detected in samples from Gazipur or Thakurgaon. Regional differences were also observed in overall virus diversity and co-infection patterns, with Dinajpur and Panchagarh showing higher numbers of detected viruses and more frequent mixed infections compared with Thakurgaon, where fewer virus species were detected. These patterns reflect regional differences in potato virus distribution in Bangladesh.

Regional differences were also observed in overall virus diversity and co-infection patterns, with Dinajpur and Panchagarh showing higher numbers of detected viruses and more frequent mixed infections compared with Thakurgaon, where fewer virus species were detected. These patterns reflect regional differences in potato virus distribution in Bangladesh.

Across all samples, mixed infections were common, with all tested samples containing two or more viruses. Viral diseases represent one of the most significant constraints to potato production globally, with substantial impacts on yield and tuber quality [ 5 ]. Although considerable progress has been made in understanding plant virus diversity and epidemiology in many crops globally [ 21 – 24 ], relatively few studies have focused on potato viruses in Bangladesh—a country where potato is vital for food security and economic stability. The viruses infecting potatoes in this region pose a major challenge to sustainable production. In this study, we provide the first comprehensive characterization of the potato virome in Bangladesh using high-throughput sequencing (HTS). This approach overcomes the limitations of earlier RT-PCR–based surveys, which were restricted to the detection of a small number of previously known viruses [ 8 ]. HTS enabled the unbiased detection of a broad range of viral pathogens, revealing both well-established potato viruses and additional viruses that had not been previously documented in the country [ 25 ]. Together, these findings substantially expand current knowledge of potato viral diversity in Bangladesh and provide a foundation for improved surveillance and management strategies.
Overall, ten viruses were detected in potato leaf samples collected from five regions of Bangladesh: PAMV, PLRV, PVA, PVV, PVX, PVY, PVS, ToCV, ToLCNDV, and PVM). All identified viruses were assembled at the complete genome level. Importantly, in all analyzed viral genomes—including bipartite genomes such as ToLCNDV (DNA-A and DNA-B) and segmented genomes such as ToCV (RNA1 and RNA2)—SNVs were broadly distributed across the entire genomic regions rather than being confined to specific loci. This genome-wide distribution pattern indicates the continuous accumulation of mutations and reflects the high genetic diversity and complex population structure of these viruses circulating across different geographic regions in Banglasdesh. Among these, potato virus V (PVV) was identified for the first time in Bangladesh and, to the best of our knowledge, has not previously been reported from South Asia. This finding represents a notable addition to the known diversity of potato viruses in the region. Subsequent RT-PCR screening across individual samples showed that several viruses were highly prevalent, particularly, PVY, ToLCNDV, and PVS. Moderate infection frequencies were observed for PVV, PAMV, ToCV, PVM, and PLRV where PVA and PVX showed comparatively lower infection rates (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). These prevalence patterns provide a clearer picture of which viruses currently dominate potato fields in Bangladesh. PVY was the most frequently detected virus in our study, which is consistent with global reports identifying PVY as the most widespread potato virus. In addition, ToLCNDV was detected in more than three-quarters of samples, indicating that this emerging virus is already widely distributed in several potato-growing regions of Bangladesh.
PVA is a member of the genus potyvirus and can cause yield losses of up to 40% in single infections. In mixed infections with other potato viruses, such as PVY or PVX, PVA has been reported to participate in synergistic interactions that result in more severe symptoms, including leaf crinkling and plant stunting and greater yield losses than those caused by individual viruses alone [ 26 – 29 ]. Although the presence of PVA has been reported previously in Bangladesh [ 8 , 30 ], this study provides the first complete genome sequence of a PVA isolate from potato in the country (the sequence is now available in NCBI; see Fig. 5 ; Table 1 ). This complete genome enables direct comparisons between the Bangladeshi PVA isolate and global strains to check for any distinct mutations or recombination events (for examples, one Chinese PVA isolate was reported to have a recombination at the 3′ end of the genome [ 27 ]. The ability to track such changes can inform whether local strains have distinct properties (e.g., more aggressive, different host range, etc.). Overall, PVA remains a known threat to potato production in Bangladesh—so continued monitoring of this virus is warranted.
PVV is a less well-studied potyvirus compared with PVY or PVA, but it has been reported in multiple regions including eight European countries, the Andean highlands of South America, parts of Central America, and the Middle East (Iran) [ 31 – 36 ]. PVV infections are often associated with mild foliar mosaic or flecking; however, the virus is more widely recognized for its ability to induce tuber necrotic rings and cracking, leading to moderate yield losses, particularly under mixed infection conditions with PVA [ 37 ]. Previous studies have suggested that PVV may be more widely distributed than currently documented, with possible but unconfirmed presence in regions such as Australasia, Africa, the Indian subcontinent, East Asia, and North America [ 38 ]. Prior to our study, PVV had not been documented in Bangladesh or to our knowledge, anywhere in South Asia [ 38 ]. Thus, our identification of PVV in Bangladesh is a significant addition to the known geographic range of this virus and a notable contribution to potato virology in the region. PVV has the potential to synergistically intensify disease symptoms in mixed infections with other potyvirus especially with PVY, as reported in other studies [ 37 – 40 ]. Our phylogenetic analysis showed that the Bangladeshi PVV isolate is nearly identical to isolates from the United Kingdom and Belgium (about 98% nucleotide similarity), hinting at a relatively recent introduction from those regions or a common source of infected seed potatoes. The phylogenetic association of the Bangladeshi isolate with European PVV strains suggests that international movement of seed potatoes or other plant materials may have contributed to its introduction. This underlines the importance of studying PVV’s epidemiology in Bangladesh. Future studies should focus on assessing its distribution, epidemiology, and potential impact on potato production. From a management perspective, now that PVV is known to be in Bangladesh, including it in diagnostic screening (e.g., during seed certification or routine virus indexing of planting material) would be prudent.
PVY is one of the most destructive and globally prevalent potato viruses. It can cause a wide array of symptoms, from mild mosaics to severe leaf necrosis and even tuber ringspot, depending on the strain (PVY has multiple strains such as PVY O , PVY N , PVY NTN , etc., which vary in symptomatology) [ 41 ]. The PVY isolate sequenced in this study (GenBank accession no. PX317648 ) showed the highest nucleotide similarity (95.83%) to a Tunisian isolate ( MG696820 ). Overall, the Bangladeshi PVY showed 85.74–95.83% identity with other PVY isolates worldwide, with BCMNV (a potyvirus of legumes) appearing as a distant outlier in our comparisons (Supplementary Figure S7). PVY is widely recognized as one of the most destructive potato viruses worldwide, particularly in agroecosystems where mixed infections are common, PVY can cause yield losses up to 80% [ 5 ]. In Bangladesh, PVY infections alone have previously been associated with roughly 45% yield reduction, especially greater losses occurring when PVY co-infects with PVX or PVA [ 42 , 43 ]. Consisting with previous findings, our results confirm that PVY remains a predominant potato virus in Bangladesh. Its high incidence and frequent involvement in co-infections (particularly with PVX, PVA, and PLRV in our observations) make it a linchpin in the potato virus disease complex. Effective management of PVY is particularly challenging due to its efficient aphid-borne transmission, the existence of multiple strains with variable pathogenicity and its capacity to interact synergistically with other viruses. The continued dominance of PVY in Bangladesh highlights the need for sustained efforts in breeding PVY-resistant cultivars and strengthening seed certification system to limit virus dissemination.
PLRV is another historically significant potato virus, known for causing “leaf roll” disease in potatoes. It is transmitted in a persistent, circulative manner by aphids (especially Myzus persicae ), meaning viruliferous aphids can transmit the virus throughout their lifetime. PLRV can cause serious yield losses—up to 50–70% in susceptible potato varieties, and losses up to 78% have been reported under severe conditions in Bangladesh [ 26 , 42 ]. The PLRV sequence characterized in this study (GenBank accession no. PX317653 ) showed very high nucleotide similarity (98.91%) to a Kenyan isolate ( MN689364 ). Global comparison showed that our PLRV falls within the known genetic diversity of PLRV, with overall nucleotide identities ranging from 96.36% to 98.91%. In the phylogenetic analysis, Cucurbit aphid-borne yellows virus (CABYV) was used as an outgroup and, as expected, was quite divergent (Supplementary Figure S4C, D). PLRV typically causes upward leaf rolling, chlorosis, and plant stunting; and like other viruses, these symptoms can become more severe in mixed infections (for example, coinfection with PVY or PVX can produce an additive effect, leading to more pronounced stunting and chlorotic netting in leaves [ 26 ]. In Bangladesh, PLRV remains a major challenge, largely due to the widespread presence of aphid vectors and difficulties in controlling those vectors [ 8 , 44 ]. The detection of PLRV across all surveyed districts (Supplementary Table S3) confirms its widespread distribution and underscores the need for improved management strategies.
PVX is generally considered mild virus, causing yield reductions of about 10–30% and mild mosaic or no visible symptoms in many potato varieties. However, its importance rises sharply in mixed infections. Co-infection with potyvirus such as PVY or PVA can result in strong synergistic interactions (potyvirus–potexvirus synergism), leading to severe mosaic, necrosis, plant deformation, and substantial yield losses [ 5 , 26 , 28 ]. PVX is globally distributed and widespread, largely due to its efficient mechanical transmission through plant handling and agricultural practices, as well as occasional nonpersistent aphid transmission. The PVX sequence from Bangladesh ( PX317649 ) was somewhat divergent compared to many reference isolates—interestingly, it had only about 76.9% identity to isolates from Japan ( AB195999 ) and Switzerland ( MF405302 ), and a slightly lower similarity to an Indian isolate ( KR605396 ). This result suggests that notable genetic divergence. Although PVX does not exhibit clearly defined strain groupings like PVY, this level of divergence highlights the genetic variability of PVX populations, as also reflected in the similarity heatmap (Supplementary Figure S6). We frequently found PVX in mixed infections in our samples, especially paired with PVY. This is consistent with the well-known PVX–PVY synergism [ 45 ]. While PVX alone may appear of minor concern, its frequent involvement in synergistic disease complexes underscores its importance in potato virus management.
PAMV has historically been reported mainly from Europe and North America but has recently gained attention following reports from parts of Asia, including China [ 46 ]. In our study, PAMV was detected at a relatively high frequency, occurring in approximately 64% of samples and frequently as part of mixed infections. Our phylogenetic analysis showed the Bangladeshi PAMV isolate is closely related to a U.S. isolate (with < 97.2% identity) and to another Bangladeshi isolate (from a separate dataset, likely another sample in our study or a previous partial sequence) (Supplementary Figure S4A). This suggests that PAMV in Bangladesh might have been introduced relatively recently (given the high similarity to the U.S. isolate) and could share a common origin with PAMV strains found overseas. Both PAMV and PVX belong to the genus Potexvirus . Although PAMV has previously been reported in Bangladesh [ 8 , 46 ], its epidemiology and impact under local production systems remain poorly understood. The frequent detection of PAMV alongside other viruses, including ToLCNDV, likely reflects shared agricultural environments rather than direct biological interaction. The availability of genome sequence data from this study will facilitate improved diagnostics and a more accurate assessment of PAMV’s role within the potato virus complex in Bangladesh.
PVS is generally considered a mild virus or latent virus, often causing no obvious symptoms, and single infections of PVS can cause yield losses of up to approximately 20% [ 26 ]. However, its significant increases in mixed infections, where PVS has been shown to contribute to enhanced disease severity and additional yield loss, particularly when co-infecting with PVX or PVM [ 26 ]. PVS is typically transmitted through infected seed tubers and mechanical and not efficiently by insects. Although PVS was first reported relatively recently in Bangladesh [ 47 ], and our results indicate that it is now widely distributed and ranks among the most prevalent potato viruses detected. The frequent detection of PVS in mixed infections in our samples indicates that it is an integral part of the potato virome here, even if by itself it might be overlooked in the absence of clear symptoms. Phylogenetic analysis showed that the Bangladeshi PVS isolate is more closely related to an isolate from Kenya (< 95.5%) and much lower similarity to one from China (< 79%), indicating the presence of distinct global lineages of PVS. These findings underscore the need for integrated management, including virus-free seed and multi-virus surveillance, to mitigate the impact of PVS and other potato viruses in the region.
PVM, a member of the genus carlavirus like PVS, is generally considered a mild or latent virus when present alone, typically causing mild mosaic or leaf crinkling in potato cultivars. Similar to PVS, PVM is primarily transmitted through infected seed tubers and mechanical means, with limited nonpersistent aphid transmission. Co-infections of PVM with viruses such as PVX or PVA can result in synergistic effects and yield loss [ 26 ]. PVM has previously been reported in Bangladesh [ 44 ], but detailed molecular information has been lacking. Our results indicate that PVM is present at moderate prevalence and frequently occurs in mixed infections with other potato viruses. Phylogenetic analysis showed that the Bangladeshi PVM sequence ( PX317652 ) is most closely related to another Bangladeshi isolate (86.5% identity) and then to a Canadian isolate (84.7%). The relatively lower identities (mid-80% range) indicate a fair amount of diversity in PVM sequences. This level of divergence is consistent with the genetic variability observed among carlaviruses, which often evolve independently in different geographic regions. The frequent detection of PVM in mixed infections suggests that, although it may be overlooked due to mild symptoms, it contributes significantly to the overall virus complex affecting potato crops in Bangladesh, highlighting the need for integrated management strategies including virus-free seed and multi-virus surveillance.
ToCV is a crinivirus that primarily infects tomato, causing a yellowing disorder, but can also infect potato as an incidental host. This virus is transmitted in a semipersistent manner by B. tabaci and other whitefly species. ToCV was originally described in tomato in the 2000s and has since spread to many tomato-producing regions worldwide [ 48 – 51 ]. The presence of ToCV in potato has previously been reported only in a limited number of countries, including Spain, Brazil [ 52 – 55 ], and our findings extend its known host-geographic range to Bangladesh. In this study, both RNA1 and RNA2 genomic segments of ToCV were detected in potato samples, confirming the presence of complete viral genome and active infection (sometimes only one segment is detected and could indicate a partial genome). Phylogenetic analysis showed that the Bangladeshi ToCV sequences clustered closely with isolates from Spain and South Africa, suggesting genetic similarity to African-Mediterranean lineages. Possibly, this strain of ToCV could have come with infected tomato or ornamental plant material, rather than evolving locally. The presence of ToCV in potatoes in Bangladesh is alarming from a biosecurity standpoint. These results highlight ToCV as an emerging component of the potato virome in Bangladesh with potential epidemiological significance.
ToLCNDV is a whitefly-transmitted begomovirus with a broad host range, infecting cucurbits and several solanaceous crops. In potato, ToLCNDV has been associated with leaf curling, yellowing, and apical leaf curl disease, which can severely affect plant growth and yield, particularly as reported from India [ 9 , 56 ]. In this study, ToLCNDV was detected at high frequency in Bangladeshi potato samples, indicating that the virus is already well established in some potato-growing regions. The detection of ToLCNDV in Bangladeshi potato fields is therefore consistent with its known regional distribution and likely reflects spread from neighboring countries. Phylogenetic analysis showed that the Bangladeshi ToLCNDV isolates clustered with isolates from China and other Bangladeshi sequences, indicating that the virus infecting potato belongs to the wider regional ToLCNDV population rather than a distinct lineage. Given its broad host range and efficient transmission by B. tabaci , ToLCNDV has strong potential to spread rapidly and persist across cropping systems. These findings highlight the need to incorporate whitefly monitoring and management into potato virus, alongside the traditional focus on aphid-transmitted viruses.
The regional variation in virus prevalence observed in this study (Fig. 4 ) indicates that potato virus incidence in Bangladesh is influenced by local environmental and agroecological conditions, including climate, cropping intensity, and vector activity [ 57 ]. Districts such as Dinajpur and Panchagarh showed higher overall virus prevalence, which may be associated with intensive cultivation systems and conditions favorable for aphid and whitefly populations, allowing sustained virus transmission. In contrast, the lower virus incidence observed in Thakurgaon may reflect fewer cropping cycles or less favorable conditions for vector establishment. Previous studies have shown that virus spread can be strongly influenced by landscape composition and insect vector communities at relatively small spatial scales and closely linked to on-farm management practices [ 58 ]. These observations suggest that locally tailored management strategies could play an important role in reducing the prevalence of potato viruses in Bangladesh.
Co-infections were more common in our study, consistent with recent HTS-based studies showing that high frequencies of mixed viral infections in potato and other crops [ 59 – 61 ]. Earlier diagnostic approaches often focused on single target viruses, which may have led to underestimation of co-infection complexity; in contrast, HTS reveals that individual plants frequently harbor multiple viruses simultaneously, such as combinations of PVY, PVS, and PLRV. Mixed infections can result in diverse biological outcomes, including neutral interactions, competitive effects, or synergistic interactions that exacerbate disease severity and yield loss. Previous studies have shown that certain virus combinations can enhance symptom severity or virus accumulation, particularly in potyvirus–potexvirus co-infections [ 62 ]. In our dataset, PVY and PLRV were frequently detected together, a combination known to be particularly damaging in potato, especially given the limited availability of cultivars with resistance to both viruses [ 63 , 64 ]. Mixed infections involving multiple viruses were therefore widespread in Dinajpur, highlighting this district as a hotspot for virus co-occurrence. Network analysis further revealed non-random co-occurrence patterns among several viruses. Clustering viruses such as PVA, PVV, and PVX may reflect shared transmission routes, including non-persistent aphid transmission and mechanical spread, rather than direct biological interactions. Similarly, co-occurrence of viruses with different primary vectors (e.g., PVM and ToCV, or PAMV and ToLCNDV) may be influenced by shared agroecosystems, mixed cropping practices, or the use of infected seed tubers combined with vector-mediated introductions. Overall, these findings indicate that potato viruses in Bangladesh frequently occur as multi-virus complexes. This complexity has important implications for disease management, as strategies targeting single viruses or individual vectors may be insufficient. Integrated approaches that combine the use of virus-free seed, vector management, and broader surveillance of multiple viruses are therefore likely to be more effective.
In conclusion, this study provides the first comprehensive overview of the potato virome in Bangladesh. Using HTS and molecular validation, we documented the dominance of well-known viruses like PVY, alongside the widespread presence of PLRV, PVS, and PVX, and importantly, the emergence of new threats including PVV (reported for the first time in Bangladesh and South Asia) and ToLCNDV in potato. The frequent occurrence of mixed infections highlights the complexity of the potato virus landscape and underscores the need to manage virus complexes rather than individual pathogens. Our work also delivered the first complete genome sequence of PVA and PVV from Bangladesh, contributing valuable genetic data for future epidemiological and evolutionary studies in South Asia.
However, our findings based on symptomatic leaves sample collected during a single season from five districts, and thus the results may not fully represent virus diversity across all potato-growing regions or over multiple seasons. In addition, the genomic information used for phylogenetic analysis was derived from consensus sequences generated from pooled samples, which may mask intra-population variability and potentially influence the interpretation of phylogenetic relationships. These constraints should be considered, and future work involving broader spatial and temporal sampling, along with biological and yield-impact studies, will be necessary to fully assess the epidemiological significance of these viruses in Bangladesh. Overall, our results emphasize the importance of integrated virus management strategies in Bangladesh, including strengthened virus surveillance, use of virus-free seed potatoes, vector management, and the development of multi-virus-resistant potato varieties, to support sustainable potato production and food security.
