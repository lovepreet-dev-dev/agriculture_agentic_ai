TITLE: High-throughput sequencing-based virome analysis reveals genomic diversity and recombination of tomato-infecting viruses in Korea
URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC13103390/

Tomatoes are one of the most economically valuable vegetable crops globally, yet viral diseases significantly hinder their production. Tomatoes are associated with the highest reported number of virus and viroid species, totaling approximately 312, but limited information exists on their diversity and prevalence in specific regions such as Korea. This study aimed to characterize the tomato virome in the Gyeongsang and Jeolla regions of Korea using high-throughput sequencing of 122 samples. Seven viruses were detected, including five previously reported—tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV), tomato chlorosis virus (ToCV), tomato spotted wilt orthotospovirus, Southern tomato virus, and pepper mild mottle virus—and two viruses previously unreported in Korea: olive latent virus 1 and tobacco necrosis virus A. TYLCV and ToCV were the most prevalent, detected in nearly all samples. Genome analysis revealed the ongoing spread of a ToCV isolate in Korea, the introduction of an overseas mild strain of TYLCV (TYLCV-Mld), and a putative lineage of TYLCV isolates resulting from interspecies recombination among begomoviruses. These findings provide critical insights into the diversity and dynamics of tomato viromes in Korea and have significant implications for disease management, underscoring the need for continued surveillance against emerging viral threats.

Tomatoes host approximately 312 known virus and viroid species, the highest number reported among all plants 1 . Viruses represent a major factor contributing to significant yield losses in tomato production 1 . Diseases caused by plant viruses often mimic physiological disorders or symptoms resulting from pesticide use, complicating early diagnosis and control. Consequently, they reduce production and lower marketability, leading to substantial economic losses 2 . These viruses are primarily transmitted via vector insects, seeds, grafting, and physical contact at agricultural sites, underscoring the need to investigate their distribution and develop effective diagnostic methods 3 . In Korea, 14 tomato-infecting viruses have been reported, including tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV) 4 , 5 , tomato mosaic virus, cucumber mosaic virus, tomato chlorosis virus (ToCV), tomato spotted wilt orthotospovirus (TSWV), southern tomato virus (STV) 6 , tomato bushy stunt virus 7 , pepper mottle virus, tomato leaf curl virus, tobacco leaf curl virus (TbLCV), tomato mottle mosaic virus, tobacco mosaic virus, tobacco mild green mosaic virus 8 , and pepino mosaic virus 9 . Among these, TYLCV, TSWV, and tomato mosaic virus are significant contributors to yield loss 10 , 11 . Co-infection with multiple pathogens is common in plants, and interactions between co-infecting pathogens play a critical role in disease distribution and severity 12 , 13 . In particular, co-infection with plant viruses is common, and complex infections are considered the rule rather than the exception in plant virus ecosystems 14 , 15 . When different viruses co-infect the same crop, at least one virus can positively influence infection processes in host plants, such as viral replication and increased cell affinity owing to the presence of different viruses 16 , 17 . Among them, complex infections with ToCV and TYLCV cause serious symptoms, and these results suggest that the two virus species have a synergistic interaction relationship 18 – 20 .
Plant viruses have been studied extensively for over a century, but most research has focused on viruses that cause visible diseases in crops. The International Committee for the Taxonomy of Viruses recognizes over 900 plant viruses, excluding many that remained unreviewed 21 . Various virus detection methods have been utilized, including traditional diagnostic tools such as inoculation of plant surfaces, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays, nucleic acid hybridization techniques, and electron microscopy. While polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and reverse transcription-PCR (RT-PCR) have enhanced virus detection through virus-specific sequences 22 , these methods rely on prior knowledge of viral sequences, making it challenging to identify unexpected or novel viruses. To address these limitations, virome analysis using high-throughput sequencing (HTS) has emerged as a powerful tool for virus detection and characterization 23 . HTS, also known as next-generation sequencing, fragments genomes into smaller segments, sequences them simultaneously, and generates vast amounts of data that are analyzed through bioinformatics 23 . This technology enables the identification of known and novel viruses without prior sequence information, allowing the reconstruction of complete or nearly complete viral genomes.
This study aims to address the gap in knowledge by investigating the genetic diversity of tomato-infecting viruses in major tomato-growing regions of Korea using HTS-based virome analysis. Specifically, we seek to identify unreported and known viruses, examine their distribution and prevalence, and provide insights into their potential implications for tomato production in Korea. By advancing our understanding of tomato viromes, this study contributes to the development of improved diagnostic tools and effective management strategies for viral diseases. Additionally, HTS-based virome analyses have been increasingly conducted by research groups worldwide. In this context, the Korean tomato virome dataset presented in this study can serve as a reference for comparative plant virus studies across different regions, climatic conditions, and cultivation systems. Furthermore, by characterizing the genotypes of tomato-infecting viruses currently circulating in Korea, this study aims to facilitate the use of its findings in future epidemiological investigations, including analyses of virus dissemination, introduction events, and population dynamics.
Tomato leaf samples were collected from 12 regions in February 2021, totaling 122 samples from Gimhae, Jangseong, Damyang, Jinju, Busan, Hadong, and Sacheon (Table S1). The samples exhibited viral disease symptoms, including yellowing, stunting, dwarfism, leaf curling, and chlorosis (Fig. 1 ). After collection, the samples were immediately placed in an ice box to preserve their integrity and subsequently stored in a deep freezer at − 80 °C for further analysis. The collected 122 samples were pooled by region for virome analysis. cDNA libraries were constructed and sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The sequencing yielded raw data ranging from 49,908,740 to 71,986,252 reads, corresponding to 5,040,782,740 to 72,270,611,452 bp (Table S2). The raw reads were processed to remove low-quality sequences and adapters using standard trimming protocols and subsequently filtered against the host genome (Fig. S1). The filtered reads were assembled into contigs, ranging from 23,826 to 204,291 per sample. BLAST analyses identified 209 contigs related to plant viruses. These contigs represented seven virus species spanning six genera, including two viruses unreported in Korea (Table 1 ): TYLCV, ToCV, STV, pepper mild mottle virus (PMMoV), TSWV, Olive latent virus 1 (OLV1), and tobacco necrosis virus A (TNVA). Among the viruses, ToCV-related contigs (123 contigs) were the most abundant, followed by TYLCV-related contigs (60 contigs), with TSWV, STV, PMMoV, OLV1, and TNVA being less frequently identified (Fig. S2). RT-PCR was conducted to validate the presence of each virus using total RNA extracted from pooled samples and specific primers targeting virus-associated sequences (Table S3). Genome reconstruction revealed that some assembled TYLCV sequences exhibited truncation in the second half of the genome, as well as numerous broken pairs and regions of low similarity in the vicinity of approximately 2000 bp of the TYLCV genome. To address this, the assembled TYLCV contigs were extended and joined to complete the 3’ region of the genome, leading to the identification of recombinant TYLCV isolates. BLASTn analysis showed that the extended 3’ region of these isolates displayed high sequence homology with Honeysuckle yellow vein virus (HYVV) and Tobacco leaf curl virus (TbLCV) (Fig. 10 ). The recombinant TYLCV isolates had genome lengths of 2771–2774 bp and contained six open reading frames, consistent with other monopartite begomoviruses (Fig. 11 ). Recombination analysis using RDP4 software validated potential recombination events between TYLCV and HYVV, with significant p-values observed across all seven detection methods (Table S8). To further validate the recombination events, RT-PCR was performed using primers designed to anneal to sequences derived from both parental virus origins (Table S9), and the expected amplicons were successfully amplified in all recombinant-positive samples (Fig. 12 ). As a result of comparing the amino acid sequences of the C4 protein from the recombinant isolate detected in this study and the Israel strain (TYLCV-Is), significant differences were identified in the N-terminal lipid modification motifs, which play a crucial role in determining subcellular localization (Fig. 13 A). Both proteins possess a Glycine (G) residue at position 2, and GPS-lipid analysis predicted that N-myristoylation occurs in both variants (Fig. 13 B). However, a distinct difference was observed in the secondary modification site, S-palmitoylation. While palmitoylation was predicted at the Cysteine (C) residue at position 8 in TYLCV-Is C4, it was determined that palmitoylation does not occur in the C4 protein of the recombinant isolate, as this residue is substituted with Serine (S). Furthermore, the prediction of the chloroplast Transit Peptide (cTP) using the ChloroP 1.1 program indicated that the cTP is absent in the C4 protein of the recombinant isolate (Fig. 13 C). Phylogenetic analysis, incorporating all TYLCV-related sequences registered in the NCBI database since this study, revealed four distinct groups: TYLCV-IL, TYLCV-Mld, and recombinant groups (Fig. 14 , Table S10). Effective detection methods are essential for controlling pathogens and ensuring sustainable crop production. However, early diagnosis and control of viral pathogens remain challenging, making it critical to investigate their detection and distribution. Tomato viruses continue to significantly impact yield and marketability, necessitating accurate and consistent surveillance for effective management.
This study investigated the population and genetic diversity of tomato-infecting viruses in Korea. A total of 122 tomato leaf samples collected across multiple regions in Korea were pooled for analysis. RNA was extracted, and the pooled samples were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The raw sequencing data, consisting of 101 bp paired-end reads, were trimmed, filtered, and assembled into contigs. These contigs were compared to viral genomes using tBLASTx and BLASTn, resulting in the identification of 209 virus-related contigs across 12 samples. Most contigs were related to TYLCV and ToCV, followed by TSWV, STV, PMMoV, OLV1, and TNVA. Seven viral species spanning six genera were identified and validated using RT-PCR.
Among the identified viruses, OLV1 and TNVA are reported in Korea for the first time. OLV1, a member of the genus Alphanecrovirus (family Tombusviridae), was first discovered in olives in Italy and subsequently isolated from tomatoes in 2010 25 . It is widespread in Mediterranean European countries and has also been identified in China ( MK376952 ) and Japan 26 . In this study, the OLV1 isolate demonstrated the highest sequence homology with the Chinese isolate ( MK376952 ). OLV1 was first identified in olive trees 27 , with its host range later expanding to include tulips, citrus fruits, and tomatoes 25 , 28 , 29 . Although this virus does not cause direct damage to tomato crops, it can be transmitted through soil in the absence of biological vectors. Given the frequent occurrence of co-infections with other viruses 30 , there is a possibility that OLV1 may recombine with other members of the Alphanecrovirus genus, potentially leading to the emergence of a new viral species 25 . Therefore, further investigation is necessary to assess the spread, epidemiology, and impact of this virus strain on tomato cultivation. Similarly, TNVA, also a member of the genus Alphanecrovirus , family Tombusviridae, is prevalent in Mediterranean regions and has been identified in China 26 . The TNVA sequence reconstructed in this study showed the highest sequence homology with a Slovenian isolate ( OL472227 ). TNVA has a broad host range 31 , 32 . However, it rarely causes systemic necrotic diseases in hosts other than Glycine max and Nicotiana benthamiana 33 , 34 , and does not pose a direct threat to tomato cultivation. Considering the potential emergence of new viral diseases associated with co-infections of alpha- and betanecrovirus in new hosts 31 , 32 , continuous monitoring and management of this virus are essential.
Complete or nearly complete genome sequences were reconstructed for TYLCV and ToCV, while partial sequences were obtained for the other viruses due to low read counts. Phylogenetic analysis of ToCV revealed that domestic isolates were divided into two clades, consistent with prior findings 24 . The Hadong 1 isolate showed regional genome reassortment, similar to the previously reported JN1 and JN2 isolates, indicating that ToCV, which had previously been introduced and reported in Korea, continues to spread within the country. In the case of TYLCV, all previously reported isolates in Korea were of the IL strain. Most TYLCV isolates identified in this study are also clustered within the TYLCV-IL group. In addition, isolates from the TYLCV-Mld group were detected, indicating the introduction of a mild strain of TYLCV into Korea. These isolates showed the highest sequence homology with a Portuguese isolate ( AF105975 ), suggesting trans-regional transmission.
As revealed by RDP4 analysis, TYLCV isolates from the newly identified group showed evidence of recombination with HYVV (or TbLCV) within the genomic region spanning approximately 2100 bp to the Ori. These isolates contained six open reading frames, consistent with other monopartite begomoviruses. Genetic distance analysis based on the Kimura-2-parameter model showed that this group exhibits a mean nucleotide divergence of 6.6% from the closest TYLCV lineages, which falls within the range commonly used by the ICTV for strain-level differentiation in begomoviruses. Accordingly, these isolates can be considered a distinct TYLCV strain candidate, pending further biological characterization. Notably, analogous recombination patterns involving the exchange of the C4 protein have been reported in several previous studies 35 – 39 . A comparable trend of recombination with HYVV was also observed in a TYLCV isolate reported in Japan in 2016 (GenBank accession no. LC202091 ). However, the minor parent sequence regions of these isolates exhibited low homology, indicating that the recombinant isolates identified in this study and those from Japan likely arose from independent recombination events. It is inferred that natural mixed infections involving these two viruses may facilitate the emergence of similar recombination patterns.
Begomoviruses are characterized by a high mutation frequency and substitution rate, akin to other RNA viruses. Recombination among begomoviruses is a well-documented mechanism that drives the diversification of disease complexes affecting various crops 40 . Such events can introduce significant genetic variations in a single instance, enhancing viral evolution by bypassing unfavorable mutations 41 . These genetic changes may manifest as phenotypic adaptations such as modulation of pathogenicity, evasion of host immune responses, and antiviral resistance. Recombination in begomoviruses can lead to changes in disease severity 42 , 43 , an expanded host range 42 , 44 , and the ability to overcome resistance 45 , 46 , making it a significant factor in the epidemic potential of these viruses. Among begomoviruses, the AC4/C4 protein is recognized as the least conserved and is often implicated as a determinant of pathogenicity. This multifunctional protein has expanding roles that continue to be elucidated 47 . For example, the C4 protein interacts with SHAGGY-like protein kinases, causing abnormal plant development 48 – 50 . It has also been identified as an inhibitor of RNA silencing 51 – 53 .
In the recombination events observed in this study, the exchange of the C4 protein between TYLCV and HYVV was identified, suggesting a potential role in altering virus virulence and suppressing host RNA silencing mechanisms. This exchange may potentially influence the pathogenicity and adaptability of recombinant isolates. Tomato yellow leaf curl China virus, which exhibits a recombination pattern highly similar to the identified TYLCV recombinant strains, acquired a more virulent C4 protein than its parental virus. This enhanced C4 protein effectively suppressed methylation and gene silencing, inducing severe developmental abnormalities. Therefore, this recombination pattern appears capable of inducing significant changes in viral virulence 39 . The C4 protein of recombinant TYLCV isolate was found to lack both the palmitoylation site (pal) 54 , which facilitates membrane association, and the chloroplast transit peptide (cTP), which mediates chloroplast targeting, compared with the wild-type virus. In apple geminivirus from China, a mutant in which the Cys8 residue responsible for palmitoylation of the C4 protein was substituted with alanine was unable to translocate to the chloroplast 55 . Various plant viruses utilize C4 proteins containing both N-myristoylation (myr) and cTP motifs to suppress host defense responses originating from the chloroplast 56 . Furthermore, TYLCV C4 protein is thought to target both the plasma membrane and the chloroplast in a context-dependent manner through dual lipid modifications, depending on the activation state of host defense responses 56 . In contrast, the C4 protein of the recombinant isolate lacks these targeting signals and may therefore be largely confined to the cytoplasm or nucleus, potentially leading to pronounced alterations in C4 subcellular localization. Such changes are expected to influence virus–host interactions. Interestingly, all samples except those from Damyang 3 and Jinju 4 tested positive for the recombinant isolates. Moreover, analysis of TYLCV sequences deposited in the NCBI database following this study suggests that these recombinant isolates have been circulating for an extended period and possess characteristics that favor their persistence and dissemination under natural conditions. Notably, the continued detection of recombinant isolates in symptomatic tomato plants indicates that these variants may overcome plant defense mechanisms through pathways distinct from those of the wild-type virus, thereby inducing disease symptoms and sustaining their spread in the field.
This study provides important insights into the diversity and genetic characteristics of tomato-infecting viruses in Korea. As high-throughput sequencing (HTS)–based virome analyses have become widely adopted, the Korean dataset presented here may serve as a valuable reference for future studies investigating virus diversity across different regions, climatic conditions, and cultivation systems. Moreover, by characterizing the genotypes of tomato-infecting viruses currently circulating in Korea, the findings of this study may be directly applicable to epidemiological investigations conducted by international research groups. However, the use of pooled samples may have limited the detection of viruses present in low abundance or restricted to specific samples. Additionally, the incomplete genomic reconstruction of certain viruses, such as STV and OLV1, could hinder a full understanding of their genetic characteristics and potential functions. The study was geographically confined to specific regions, and broader sampling across other tomato-growing areas in Korea would be necessary to capture a more comprehensive virome profile. Future studies should incorporate long-term surveys across multiple cultivation periods and expanded geographic sampling to more systematically evaluate the temporal and spatial dynamics of tomato viromes. In parallel, further research is needed to investigate the biological impact of recombinant TYLCV isolates, particularly with respect to their virulence, host adaptation, and interactions with resistant cultivars. Longitudinal studies could provide insights into the environmental persistence and spread of these isolates, while experimental infection models may help elucidate the functional consequences of recombination events, including potential alterations in C4 protein activity. By addressing these gaps, future research will contribute to a deeper understanding of the evolutionary dynamics of tomato-infecting viruses and support the development of more effective disease management strategies.
In February 2021, tomato leaves exhibiting viral symptoms were collected from the Gyeongsang and Jeolla regions to assess the distribution of viruses in domestically grown tomatoes. Sampling was conducted in 12 areas, including Gimhae, Jangseong, Damyang, Jinju, Busan, Hadong, and Sacheon, targeting regions where tomato cultivation was prevalent and viral diseases had been recently reported (Fig. S3). Tomato leaf samples were collected from commercial fields with permission obtained from the field owners/farm managers. No protected or endangered plant species were sampled, and no specific permits were required for the collection of cultivated tomato material. Each collected sample was stored individually at − 80 °C to preserve RNA integrity for subsequent analysis.
The collected samples were pooled into 12 groups based on their respective regions. Total RNA was extracted from these pooled samples using the NucleoSpin RNA Plant Kit (Macherey–Nagel, Düren, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Plant Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), following the guideline outlined in the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Reference Guide (1000000040499v00). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating 101 bp paired-end reads. RNA quality control, library generation, and sequencing were outsourced to Macrogen, Inc. (Seoul, South Korea).
The raw sequencing reads were analyzed using CLC Genomics Workbench v.21.0.1 (Qiagen Bioinformatics, Hilden, Germany). Reads were processed to remove low-quality sequences and adapters using the “trim reads” tool, applying a quality limit of 0.05 and a minimum length of 50 nucleotides. To exclude host transcripts, unmapped reads were extracted by comparing them to the Solanum lycopersicum reference genome (GCF_000188115.5_SL3.1_genomic) using the “map reads to reference” tool (length fraction 0.5, similarity fraction 0.8). The filtered reads were assembled into contigs using the “De novo assembly” tool, applying a length fraction of 0.5, a similarity fraction of 0.8, and a minimum contig length of 200 bp (word size, 20; bubble size, 50). The assembled contigs were subjected to BLAST analysis to detect plant virus-related contigs. Contigs exceeding 200 nt were annotated using BLAST and tBLASTx searches against local (NCBI viral refseq, 2022) and online virus, viroid, and nt/nr databases, with an e-value cut-off of 1 × 10 –4 . Plant virus-related contigs were identified based on sequence homology, and trimmed reads were subsequently remapped to the sequence with the highest homology to reconstruct complete or nearly complete genomes. Viral read counts were also measured to quantify the abundance of each detected virus.
Virus-specific primers were used for RT-PCR validation (Tables S3 and S9). Viral DNA and RNA were extracted from the collected samples using a Viral Gene-spin Viral DNA/RNA Extraction Kit (iNtRON Biotechnology, Seongnam, Korea) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Viral DNA and RNA were amplified using the SuPrime-Script RT-PCR Kit (GeNet Bio, Daejeon, Korea). The RT-PCR conditions were as follows: stage 1, 50 °C for 30 min; stage 2, 94 °C for 5 min; stage 3, 35 cycles each of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 60 s; and stage 4, 72 °C for 5 min. The RT-PCR products were electrophoresed on a 1% agarose gel.
The expanded genomes of TYLCV and ToCV, either complete or nearly complete, were aligned with sequences from domestic and international isolates using MUSCLE (Tables S6–S8) 57 . The aligned sequences were used to construct phylogenetic trees using SplitsTree App and the Maximum Likelihood method in MEGA 11, with the Kimura 2-parameter model applied 58 , 59 . To ensure the reliability of the phylogenetic tree, bootstrap analysis was conducted with 1000 replicates to minimize potential errors during systematic number preparation. Pairwise comparison tables were generated using SDT v1.3 software, based on the sequences used in the phylogenetic analysis 60 . The relationships among different sequences and the number of nucleotide substitutions between them were further evaluated using the Templeton, Crandall, and Sing (TCS) method 61 . Aligned sequences were subjected to statistical parsimony analysis using TCS (v.1.21) implemented in the software Population Analysis with Reticulate Trees (POPART). The N-terminal N-myristoylation and S-palmitoylation sites of TYLCV C4 protein were predicted using the GPS-lipid 1.0 (Group-based Prediction System for Lipid Modification) server 62 . A high-stringency cutoff was applied to ensure the reliability of the predicted lipid modification scores. The presence and length of the chloroplast transit peptide (cTP) were analyzed using the ChloroP 1.1 server 63 . The prediction was based on the presence of specific N-terminal presequences, with results categorized by the final score and cleavage site probability.
The reference sequences for TYLCV and HYVV, along with the sequences of putative recombinant isolates detected in this study, were analyzed for evidence of interspecies recombination using the Recombination Detection Program, version 4.101 (RDP4) 64 . Recombination analysis utilized multiple algorithms integrated into RDP4, including the Recombination Detection Program (RDP), GENECOV, Max Chi, Chimaera, SiScan, and 3Seq. Recombination events were deemed statistically significant if they were identified by at least four distinct methods and had a p-value below the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold of 0.05.
