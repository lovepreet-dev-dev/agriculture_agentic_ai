TITLE: Integrated transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics analyses reveal key regulatory pathways related to the development of viral symptoms in tomato plants
URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC13122918/

Viral infections of tomato plants present a significant threat to tomato production and yield. Mixed viral infections are common in field-grown tomatoes and are known to exacerbate disease symptoms; however, the molecular mechanisms affecting symptom severity remain poorly understood. This study used integrated omics analyses to identify tissue-specific molecular pathways associated with severe and mild disease symptoms in tomato plants naturally co-infected with multiple viruses.
Our analyses revealed multiple pathways that were differentially expressed in tomato plants in response to viral disease. Among them, the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis enrichment pathway (ko00940) is associated with leaf symptoms, stem growth, and fruit-set rate, and is a key enrichment pathway in the development of viral disease symptoms in tomato plants. In addition, molecules of the porphyrin metabolism enrichment pathway (ko00860) were highly expressed in mature tomato leaf tissues with mild viral disease, which corresponded to better leaf development. qRT-PCR showed that expression of key genes in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and the porphyrin metabolism enrichment pathways differed among tissue types of tomato plants with severe and mild viral disease, and the trend was consistent with the transcriptome results. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes across three aggregation modules were significantly correlated with the severity of tomato viral disease symptoms. Conventional virus detection assays confirmed mixed infections with tomato leaf curl New Delhi virus, tomato chlorosis virus, and tomato mosaic virus in tomato plants exhibiting viral symptoms.
We present a multi-tissue, multi-omics systems view of mixed viral infections in a field setting, which provides a valuable reference for screening and exploring key regulatory molecular functions and mechanisms underlying the development of viral disease symptoms.

Plants in growth environments are typically co-infected with multiple viruses [ 1 , 2 ], and tomato plants are susceptible to hundreds of viruses [ 3 ]. Combined infection with tomato chlorosis virus (ToCV) and tomato spotted wilt virus (TSWV) compromises the resistance of tomato plants to TSWV [ 4 ]. The accumulation of ToCV in plants with mixed infection is significantly higher; however, the viruses remained confined to the phloem and did not spread into other tissues [ 4 ]. Mixed infection with the two viruses accelerates death of tomato plants [ 4 ]. Similarly, co-infection with tomato leaf curl New Delhi virus (ToLCNDV) and tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV) exacerbates viral disease symptoms compared with single-virus infections [ 5 ]. ToLCNDV accumulation is higher in co-infected than in single-infected tomato plants [ 5 ]. Additionally, TYLCV and TSWV reportedly co-infect tomato plants [ 6 , 7 ]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the detrimental effects of mixed infection on plant resistance and aggravating symptom severity remain to be elucidated.
The development of omics technologies has facilitated the use of joint omics to explore complex phenotypes. Furthermore, transcriptome analysis is effective for investigating the regulation of gene expression in organisms [ 8 , 9 ]. Proteomics allows for qualitative and quantitative analyses of proteins in various tissues and comparison of expression differences at the protein level [ 10 , 11 ]. Metabolomics, facilitated by high-throughput processes such as liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), can be used to detect various metabolite levels in plant tissues [ 12 , 13 ]. Integrating multiple-omics data enables the systematic analysis of phenotypic differences in viral infections.
Integrated omics approached to analyze molecular mechanisms has facilitated considerable advancements regarding plant responses to biotic and abiotic stresses. Transcriptome and proteome analyses have revealed the temporal expression specificity of key genes and proteins during oil-tea camellia seed maturation [ 10 ]. Similarly, integrated transcriptome, proteome, and metabolome analyses have been used to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying various plant responses, including the decline in apple quality under high-nitrogen conditions [ 14 ], changes in litchi pulp quality under normal-temperature storage conditions [ 15 ], and common bean responses to cold stress [ 16 ]. These approaches have been used to investigate the growth status and metabolite accumulation of astragalus during different harvest periods [ 17 ], histone modification (crucial for strong light-induced changes in stomatal density and secondary metabolites) in the tea tree [ 18 ], and maize root responses to drought stress [ 19 ]. Integrated analysis of transcriptome and metabolome data has revealed that octoploid strawberry plants can resist Colletotrichum fructicola infection by regulating the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway [ 20 ]. At present, the application of integrated analyses in the field of virus infection is lacking, and the relevance of key regulatory pathways to viral disease symptoms in tomato plants remains to be comprehensively explored.
Tomatoes are essential vegetable crops that are consumed worldwide and prized for their high nutritional value. However, field-grown tomato plants are typically infected with various viruses. Some tomato plants infected with multiple viruses exhibit severe symptoms, including leaf yellowing and curling, plant dwarfing, and low seed setting rate, which significantly reduce yield and quality; symptom severity may vary considerably, as even plants of the same variety infected with multiple viruses may exhibit mild symptoms in various tissues and maintain normal fruit production and quality [ 3 , 21 ]. Therefore, we analyzed the molecular characteristics underlying the differences in symptom severity of tomato plants co-infected with multiple viruses in the field using integrated omics approaches. Our insights constitute a valuable reference for understanding the development and regulation mechanisms of viral disease symptoms in tomato plants.
Tomato plants ( Solanum lycopersicum L.) were obtained from the vegetable base of Weifang University of Science and Technology in Shouguang, China. A common production variety (Yulinglong) was used, grown in one plot with no variation in environmental conditions. After 70 days of field cultivation, tomato plants with mild and severe viral disease symptoms were selected. Leaves of plants with mild virus disease (MS plant) unfolded normally, and plant height and fruit development was normal (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Tomato leaves with severe viral disease (SS plant) were severely yellowed and curled, the plants were dwarfed, and the seed setting rate was low (Supplementary Fig. 1b). The plant height of tomato plants with mild viral disease was approximately 220 cm, and the plant height with dwarfed growth was approximately 60 cm (Supplementary Fig. 1). Plants with mild virus disease exhibited an average fruit setting rate of six, and fruits turned red; the fruit setting rate of tomato plants with severe virus disease was one, on average, and fruits remained green (Supplementary Fig. 1). The following tissue types were sampled from each plant: new leaves (second expanded leaf from the top) (XSYP), mature leaves (seventh expanded leaf from the top) (CSYP), stem segments from the middle section (cut into small, equidistant pieces; the stem segment tissue) (JD), and fruits (cut into small pieces; the fruit tissue) (GS). The sampled plant tissues were frozen in liquid nitrogen and transported to Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. for omics analysis.
Total RNA was extracted from samples (Invitrogen, Shanghai, China), and RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 analyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). A transcriptome library was constructed using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The constructed library was qualified using the Agilent 2100 analyzer and sequenced using an Illumina Novaseq 6000 sequencing platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. Low-quality reads were removed using fastp software, and clean reads were processed for subsequent data analysis [ 22 ]. The reference genome ( https://solgenomics.net/ftp/tomato_genome/assembly/build_4.00/S_lycopersicum_chromosomes.4.00.fa ) was aligned using HISAT2 software, gene expression levels were calculated, and read counts of each gene were obtained using HTSeq-count [ 23 – 25 ]. R software was used to perform principal component analysis (PCA) and gene mapping to evaluate sample biological duplication. Differentially expressed genes were analyzed using DESeq2 software, and genes that met the q value of 2 or a fold change < 0.5 threshold were defined as differentially expressed genes [ 26 ]. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was performed on the differentially expressed genes using a hypergeometric distribution algorithm to identify significantly enriched functional items [ 27 , 28 ].
An appropriate amount of sample was removed, fully ground, and transferred to a 1.5-mL centrifuge tube, and the sample lysate (CWBIO, Beijing, China; CW0885M) was added to extract the total protein. After enzymatic hydrolysis, the peptides were desalted using a SOLATM SPE 96-well plate. The peptides were eluted and vacuum-dried for subsequent use. Before mass spectrometry injection, each sample was mixed with iRT peptides (1:20 volume ratio) as an internal standard. After enzymatic hydrolysis, samples were separated using the EASY-nLC 1200 liquid chromatography system with a gradient elution. The gradient elution conditions were as follows: approximately 0–20 min, 5%–22% B; 20–24 min, 22%–37% B; 24–27 min, 37%–80% B; 27–30 min, 80% B. The mass spectrometry retrieval parameters were as follows: pre-mass threshold, 0.01; protein mass threshold, 0.01; fixed modification, carbamidomethyl (C); variable modification, oxidation (M) and acetyl (N-term); maximum missing site, two. Proteins with fold changes ≥ 2 and p values < 0.05 were considered significantly upregulated, whereas those with fold changes ≤ 0.5 and p values < 0.05 were considered significantly downregulated. The KEGG database was used to analyze the primary pathways involved in differential proteins.
The samples were ground with a methanol-water solution (4:1, v/v, containing 4 µg/mL L-2-chlorophenylalanine) and extracted ultrasonically in an ice water bath for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and transferred to an LC-MS injection vial for evaporation. Subsequently, an appropriate amount of methanol-water (1:4, v/v) was added to the injection vial for re-dissolution. Next, the mixture was filtered using a 0.22-µm organic phase pinhole filter for subsequent LC-MS analysis. Quality control samples were prepared by mixing equal volumes of all sample extracts. An LC-MS system comprising ACQUITY UPLC I-Class plus ultra-high performance liquid chromatography tandem QE plus a high-resolution mass spectrometer was used to complete the detection of metabolites.
The gradient elution system comprised (A) water (containing 0.1% formic acid, v/v) and (B) acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid, v/v) with the following gradient: 0.01 min, 5% B; 2 min, 5% B; 4 min, 30% B; 8 min, 50% B; 10 min, 80% B; 14 min, 100% B; 15 min, 100% B; 15.1 min, 5% B; 16 min, 5% B. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min, and the column temperature was 45 °C. All samples were stored at 10 °C during analysis.
The LC-MS data were processed using Progenesis QI V2.3 software (Nonlinear, Dynamics, Newcastle, UK) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, peak alignment, and normalization. The data matrix was imported into R software for PCA to test the overall distribution between the samples and the stability of the entire analysis process. Orthogonal partial and partial least squares discriminant analyses were used to distinguish differences in metabolites between groups. A two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to verify the significance of metabolite differences between groups. Differential metabolites with p values < 0.05 were selected.
Co-significant enrichment analysis of KEGG pathways was conducted by integrating KEGG enrichment results from the original omics report. Significantly enriched pathways (with p values < 0.05) in each group were selected for intersection analysis, and KEGG pathways that were co-significantly enriched were extracted. A significance histogram of the KEGG pathway was created by constructing a histogram of the top 30 common pathways in each group and marking the significance threshold line to visualize the significance distribution of the common pathways in each group. A KEGG pathway feature heatmap was generated to reveal the characteristic expression patterns of the intersection pathways in each group, based on the results of the pathway intersection analysis. The expression value distribution of the features of the 10 most significant intersection paths was constructed as a default setting (all intersection paths are drawn when the intersection is < 10). Ten features with the most significant differences (p-value ranking) in each group were selected by default for the name display. KEGG pathway mapping analysis was used to map the differentials of each group to the KEGG pathway database [ 29 ] to obtain common pathway information. Pathways with simultaneous multi-omics differences were selected and colored.
Total RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis were performed as described previously [ 30 ]. cDNA obtained from plant total RNA was amplified by PCR using a 2× Taq Master Mix (P112; Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR products were electrophoresed on 1% agarose gel at 80 V for 40 min, and gels were examined using UV light to analyze the products. qRT-PCR was completed as described previously [ 30 ]. The selection of enrichment pathway detection genes was based on the significance of differential expression in the transcriptome and the importance of coding products in the enrichment pathway. EF1ɑ and actin were selected as internal reference genes. The primer sequences and further details are provided in Additional file 1.
The gene co-expression network was constructed using the WGCNA package in R software (version 3.5.1) according to the official WGCNA tutorial. Initially, clustering analysis was performed after excluding samples exhibiting a low correlation or failing to cluster on the dendrogram. To satisfy the scale-free topology criterion, the soft-thresholding power was determined using the “pickSoftThreshold” function by selecting the parameter at which the scale-free topology fit index first approached 0.8. When R-square reached 0.8, the minimum power was selected; if none of the tested power values reached 0.8, the maximum power value was chosen. Subsequently, a correlation-based association between phenotypes and gene modules was calculated to generate an adjacency matrix, which was transformed into a topological overlap matrix to construct the gene co-expression network. Gene modules were identified and clustered using the dynamic tree cut method based on module eigengenes, with modules exhibiting high similarity. The module similarity threshold was set to 0.25 and minimum module size to 30 genes.
All experiments were repeated using at least three replicates. Data are presented as means ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed using t tests in GraphPad Prism.
Mature leaves, new leaves, stem segments, and fruits of tomato plants with severe and mild viral diseases were used for transcriptome detection. Over 19,000 genes were detected in each group (Supplementary Fig. 2a; Additional files 2 and 3). PCA showed that the distribution of each group was compact, reflecting good repeatability (Supplementary Fig. 2b). The correlation test and cluster analysis between samples reflected the different relationships among the four organizations (Fig. 1 a and Supplementary Fig. 2c). The histogram of differentially expressed genes showed that 1,489, 1,842, 2,367, and 1,687 genes were upregulated and 299, 1,673, 1,444, and 2,292 genes were downregulated in mature leaves, new leaves, stems, and fruits of tomato plants with severe viral disease, respectively, compared with tomato plants with mild viral disease (Fig. 1 b, Additional file 4). A Venn diagram showed the intersection of differentially expressed genes between the groups, with 255 response genes co-regulated (Fig. 1 c; Additional file 5). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed 109, 124, 129, and 113 functional entries with differential expression in the mature leaves, new leaves, stem segments, and fruits of tomato plants with severe viral diseases and tomato plants with mild viral diseases, respectively (Fig. 2 a–d; Additional files 6–9). These findings validate the reliability of transcriptome detection and highlight the differences and connections between groups, revealing the enrichment item information related to the formation of differential phenotypes, which provides a basis for further omics joint analysis.

Proteomic analysis revealed 572, 413, 326, and 234 significantly upregulated and 430, 190, 296, and 38 significantly downregulated proteins in mature leaves, new leaves, stems, and fruits, respectively, of tomato plants with severe viral disease compared with tomato plants with mild viral disease (Fig. 3 a–d; Additional files 10–13). Additionally, metabolome analysis revealed 1,028, 864, 508, and 525 significantly upregulated and 155, 213, 96, and 76 significantly downregulated metabolites in mature leaves, new leaves, stems, and fruits, respectively, of tomato plants with severe viral disease compared with tomato plants with mild viral disease (Fig. 3 e–h; Additional files 14–17). KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the mature leaves, new leaves, stems, and fruits of tomato plants with severe viral disease had 8 (ko00195, ko00196, ko00710, ko00860, ko00480, ko00630, ko00030, ko00250), 5 (ko00940, ko00073, ko04626, ko00350, ko04016), 6 (ko00520, ko03030, ko00591, ko00100, ko00240, ko00940), and 7 (ko00520, ko04016, ko04626, ko01040, ko00480, ko00901, ko00940) enrichment items, respectively, overlapping at the nucleic acid and protein levels compared with tomato plants with mild viral disease; 1 (ko00906), 0, 1 (ko00940), and 2 (ko00999, ko00940) enrichment items overlapped at the nucleic acid and metabolite levels, respectively (Fig. 3 i–l; Additional file 18–25). Notably, the stem segments of tomato plants with severe viral disease had a common enrichment item (ko00940) at the nucleic acid, protein, and metabolite levels compared with those of tomato plants with mild viral disease (Fig. 3 k; Additional file 26). Similarly, the fruits of tomato plants with severe viral disease had a common enrichment item (ko00940) at the nucleic acid, protein, and metabolite levels compared with those of tomato plants with mild viral disease (Fig. 3 l; Additional file 27). These results provide data for analyzing molecular characteristics related to the development of tomato plant viral disease symptoms.

Heatmap analysis of the KEGG pathway characteristics revealed that the differentially expressed genes, proteins, and metabolites in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis enrichment pathway in fruit tissues of tomato plants with severe viral disease were consistently upregulated compared with those in fruit tissues of tomato plants with mild viral disease (Fig. 5 e; Additional files 38–43). To systematically identify co-expressed gene modules and candidate genes associated with differences in viral disease symptoms, a weighted gene co-expression network was constructed based on 15,521 high-expression change genes in four tissues (Additional file 44). The soft threshold power value of 30 was selected to meet the scale-free network distribution characteristics as closely as possible (Fig. 7 a; Additional file 45). A gene clustering tree was constructed and divided into 15 co-expression modules through dynamic cutting (Additional file 46). Each module is shown in a different color; the pink module contained the largest number of genes (6286), the antiquewhite1 module contained the smallest number (27), and the gray module contained genes that could not be classified into one of the above modules (434) (Fig. 7 b). Correlation analysis of each module showed that the antiquewhite2 and darkseagreen3 modules showed a highly significant positive correlation with severe tomato virus disease symptoms (leaf curling, stem dwarfing, and low seed setting rate; Fig. 7 c; Additional file 47). The navajowhite module also showed a significant positive correlation with these symptoms (Fig. 7 c; Additional file 47). According to the results of module division, cluster analysis was performed on all 15,521 differential genes (Supplementary Fig. 3a; Additional file 46). The 63, 792 and 41 differential genes of the antiquewhite2, darkseagreen3, and navajowhite modules were also extracted for cluster analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3b-d; Additional files 48–50). Cluster analysis showed comparative information of gene expression. KEGG analysis showed that the genes in the three differential modules were enriched in 14, 20, and 6 pathways, respectively (Fig. 7 d-f; Additional files 51–53). The phenylpropanoid biosynthesis enrichment pathway was included in the darkseagreen3 module. Based on the the WGCNA results, a core gene interaction network of the antiquewhite2, darkseagreen3, and navajowhite modules was constructed showing genes with high connectivity in the module (Fig. 7 g-i; Additional files 54–56). These clustering modules and differential gene information will provide a basis for further exploring the molecular mechanism of tomato severe viral disease symptom formation.

We mixed the cDNA of four tissue samples of tomato plants with severe viral disease and tomato plants with mild viral disease and performed PCR detection to determine the common tomato virus species that may participate in these mixed infections. Five primer sets for detecting common viruses, including ToLCNDV, ToCV, tomato mosaic virus (ToMV), TSWV, and chilli ringspot virus, were used to identify tomato viruses. ToLCNDV, ToCV, and ToMV amplification bands were detected in tomato plants with severe viral disease and those with mild viral disease (Supplementary Fig. 4a). According to the brightness of the bands, it can be seen that the virus accumulation in mild tomato plants was less than that in severe tomato plants. In the two groups of tomato plants, the detected bands of ToLCNDV and ToMV were bright, while the detected bands of ToCV were weak. This result was verified by qRT-PCR, and the difference in virus accumulation between the two groups of tomato plants was quantified (Supplementary Fig. 4b). These results show that the observed virus disease symptoms were associated with the mixed infection of ToLCNDV, ToCV, and ToMV.
Previous studies have revealed the multifaceted role of the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway in the development and stress responses of plants. In banana, low-temperature stress induces the expression of MaMYB13, activating the transcription of genes related to the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway and enhancing cold tolerance in fruits [ 31 ]. In contrast, in potatoes, low temperatures may inhibit the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, affecting wound responses [ 32 ]. Furthermore, the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway interacts with melatonin to improve salt tolerance in common beans during the sprouting stage [ 33 ]. Soybean GmESR1 inhibits the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway and increases the protein content of soybean seeds [ 34 ]. The phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway is also closely associated with the strawberry ripening process [ 35 ]. In bamboo, organic fertilizers can inhibit the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway and regulate secondary cell wall biosynthesis in internodes [ 36 ]. Additionally, products of this pathway contribute to insect resistance in tomato [ 37 ]. The porphyrin metabolism enrichment pathway is related to photosynthesis [ 38 ].
We found that the molecular expression level of the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis enrichment pathway was correlated with the viral disease symptoms in new leaves, stem growth, and seed setting rate of tomato plants. The high-level expression of molecules in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis enrichment pathway may help new leaves of tomato plants resist virus infection. The molecular expression levels in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway was reduced in stem dwarf tomato plants. Additionally, the high-level expression of molecules in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis enrichment pathway was associated with low seed setting rates in tomato plants, which may be a survival strategy by trading off seed setting to maintain survival under severe viral disease. The molecular expression level of the porphyrin metabolism enrichment pathway was inhibited in mature leaves of tomato plants with severe viral disease symptoms, which may be related to leaf curl and reduced photosynthesis symptoms. The phenylpropanoid biosynthesis enrichment pathway is directly related to lignin synthesis, which plays a crucial role in plant resistance to viral infections [ 39 ]. A limitation of our study is that the applicability of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis enrichment pathway and porphyrin metabolism enrichment pathway may be limited to specific mixed-infection virus diseases and symptoms. In addition to ToLCNDV, ToCV, and ToMV, the mixed-infection viruses observed in this study may co-occur with other pathogens which were not detected; however, virus types affecting tomato plants among similar growth environments can be assumed to be similar. The differentially expressed molecules in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis enrichment pathway and the porphyrin metabolism enrichment pathway will be target references for exploring the specific mechanism underlying the development of viral disease symptoms in tomato plants.
Our integrated omics approach revealeds phenylpropanoid biosynthesis enrichment pathway as a conserved, multi-tissue regulatory hub for systemic symptom development, while porphyrin metabolism enrichment pathway disruption is a leaf-specific contributor to photosynthetic symptoms. WGCNA found three gene co-expression modules related to tomato viral disease symptoms. These insights provide a basis for key regulatory molecular screening, functional verification, and mechanistic investigations in future studies.
