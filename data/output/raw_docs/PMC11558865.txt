TITLE: Multi-kernel inception aggregation diffusion network for tomato disease detection
URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11558865/

Tomato leaf diseases significantly impact the yield and quality of tomatoes during cultivation, the main of which are septoria leaf spot, leaf curl virus, verticillium wilt, and early blight. These diseases necessitate prompt detection and management strategies to mitigate their deleterious effects on crop productivity. Due to the considerable scale variations in diseased tomato leaves, accurate and rapid detection and diagnosis remain challenging. To address the detection of tomato leaf diseases at different scales, we propose a real-time detection model incorporating a Multi-kernel Inception Aggregation Diffusion Network. In this paper, (1) We present a Multi-kernel Inception Aggregation Diffusion Network (MIADN) for the feature processing stage, which facilitates the aggregation and diffusion of multi-scale features across hierarchical levels, benefiting the detection of targets at various scales. (2) We present the Multi-kernel Inception Module (MKIM), designed to extract multi-scale object features using diverse convolution kernels, thereby enhancing the model’s feature fusion and representation capabilities. (3) We incorporate the efficient FasterNet network at the feature extraction stage to preserve feature diversity and improve the model’s ability to extract complex target features. (4) Extensive comparative and ablation experiments demonstrate that our method achieves the mean average precision (mAP50) of 96.6%, surpassing the baseline model by 4.1% and the advanced YOLOv9s model by 2.0%. This method provides an effective solution for high-quality tomato cultivation.
Tomatoes are not only an indispensable vegetable in daily diets but also play a crucial role in human nutrition due to their rich content of vitamins, minerals, and antioxidants. With the continuous growth of the global population, the demand for tomatoes has been steadily increasing, underscoring the economic and social significance of ensuring high yields and quality in tomato cultivation. However, climate change-induced extreme weather events, fluctuations in humidity, and rising temperatures have exacerbated the frequency and severity of tomato diseases (Fig. 1 ), introducing greater uncertainties for farmers and agricultural production [ 1 ]. Common tomato diseases include septoria leaf spot, leaf curl, leaf verticillium wilt, and leaf blight. These diseases not only impede the normal growth of tomatoes but also reduce their photosynthetic efficiency and hinder nutrient absorption, ultimately leading to significant yield reductions [ 2 ]. septoria leaf spot disease results in numerous lesions on the leaves, compromising photosynthetic capacity. Leaf curl disease causes leaf deformation and curling, disrupting the plant’s normal metabolic functions. Leaf verticillium wilt, which infects through the root system, leads to plant wilting and death. Leaf blight causes leaf desiccation and defoliation, severely affecting tomato yield and quality [ 3 ]. The presence of these diseases not only directly weakens the growth capacity of tomato plants but can also result in large-scale crop losses, significantly impacting economic returns. Therefore, it is crucial to detect diseases in their early stages and implement control measures to prevent widespread outbreaks, thereby ensuring healthy growth and high-quality yields of tomatoes [ 4 ]. This approach is vital for maintaining sustainable tomato production in the face of increasing environmental challenges and growing global demand.

Traditional crop disease detection methods primarily rely on field inspections conducted by agricultural experts or surveyors (Fig. 2 ). These approaches, while requiring substantial time, effort, and expertise, are also prone to errors such as misdiagnosis or the overlooking of diseases [ 5 ]. For instance, a study on conventional disease detection methods revealed that even experienced agricultural experts might have a misdiagnosis rate of approximately 15% when identifying diseases with similar symptoms [ 6 ]. Furthermore, the efficiency of traditional methods is insufficient to meet the demand for rapid disease diagnosis in large-scale agricultural production [ 1 ]. With the rapid advancement of computer vision and machine learning technologies, automated crop disease detection methods based on these advanced techniques have emerged, offering new possibilities to address the limitations of traditional approaches. Early research primarily focused on using machine learning algorithms for crop disease classification. For example: Barbedo et al. reviewed the applications of machine learning in plant pathology, highlighting the potential and challenges of image classification in plant disease diagnosis [ 7 ]. Waghmare et al. developed a system based on image processing and machine learning for detecting and classifying diseases on grape leaves [ 8 ]. Ramcharan et al. developed a convolutional neural network-based system for identifying three major cassava diseases [ 9 ]. Dhingra et al. proposed a SVM-based approach for identifying multiple diseases in tomato leaves [ 10 ]. Türkoğlu and Hanbay developed a method based on deep feature extraction and SVM classification for identifying diseases on plant leaves [ 11 ]. Sunil et al. combined classical deep learning models such as AlexNet, ResNet, and VGGNet to perform classification detection tasks on several plant leaf datasets [ 12 ]. Sunil et al. present a systematic review of deep learning and machine learning-based methods for plant disease detection and classification [ 13 ]. C.K. et al. used ResNet50, MFFN, and adaptive attention mechanism to fuse channel, spatial, and pixel-level attention for the classification of tomato leaves [ 14 ]. C.K. et al. utilized AlexNet, ResNet50, and VGG16 models for binary and multiple classification detection on a variety of plant leaf datasets [ 15 ]. Sunil et al. achieved excellent results in the classification of cardamom leaf diseases using U²-Net and EfficientNetV2 [ 16 ]. These studies demonstrate the increasing application and efficacy of machine learning and computer vision techniques in crop disease detection, offering promising alternatives to traditional methods.

However, these models mainly focus on disease classification and lack the ability to recognize disease location and size, which limits their effectiveness in practical applications. Consequently, they could not be effectively applied to disease detection and prevention tasks for tomatoes and other vegetables. In subsequent years, a new class of deep learning-based solutions emerged, aiming to capture more comprehensive characteristics of crop diseases while simultaneously providing information on disease category, location, and extent. These methods primarily leveraged object detection and semantic segmentation techniques. For instance, Liu et al. used deep mobile networks for early identification of tomato gray spot disease and achieved good results [ 17 ]. Liu et al. proposed a deep learning-based apple disease detection method that could simultaneously perform disease classification and localization [ 18 ]. Jiang et al. developed a Mask R-CNN-based system for identifying and segmenting multiple diseases on apple leaves [ 19 ]. Altuntaş et al. introduced a method based on convolutional neural networks and transfer learning for identifying diseases on plant leaves [ 20 ]. Li et al. proposed a deep learning-based approach for identifying rice diseases, capable of localization even in complex backgrounds [ 21 ]. Li et al. used a deep transfer learning approach for tea disease recognition [ 22 ]. Tang et al. proposed an accurate image-based tomato leaf disease detection method based on PLPNet [ 23 ]. These advanced approaches demonstrate a shift towards more comprehensive disease detection systems, addressing the limitations of earlier classification-only models by incorporating localization and severity assessment capabilities.
While these deep learning-based methods have made significant progress in crop disease recognition, capable of simultaneous disease classification and localization, they still face limitations in terms of accuracy and real-time performance. In recent years, an advanced real-time object detection model, You Only Look Once (YOLO), has emerged. This model offers advantages in both real-time processing and high accuracy, leading to its widespread application in crop disease detection. For instance, Liu et al. proposed a YOLOv3-based tomato disease detection method that demonstrated good performance in actual field environments [ 3 ]. Wang et al. used the optimized YOLOv3 to detect 9 tomato pests and diseases with an accuracy rate of up to 91.81% [ 24 ]. Li et al. introduced a rice disease detection method combining YOLOv5 with attention mechanisms, enhancing detection accuracy [ 25 ]. Li et al. employed an improved YOLOv5 model for grape disease detection, exhibiting excellent performance in multi-scale object detection [ 26 ]. Chen et al. developed a miniaturized crop disease detection model based on YOLOv5, suitable for edge computing devices [ 27 ]. Sun et al. designed a lightweight model YOLO-FMDI to solve the problem of tomato pest and disease detection with an detection accuracy of 93.9 [ 28 ]. Lin et al. proposed a dual-branch network named LGNet with local and global feature perception for plant disease detection [ 29 ]. Jiang et al. combined YOLOv8 and Swin Transformer to expand the feature receptive field of cabbage and improve the performance of YOLOv8-cabbage [ 30 ]. Luo et al. introduced the Light-SA YOLOv8 model to improve the accuracy and computational efficiency of citrus pest and disease detection [ 31 ]. Dong et al. designed the YOLOv8n-vegetable model to address challenges related to inaccurate detection of vegetable diseases in greenhouse plant environments using existing network models [ 32 ]. These studies demonstrate the evolving landscape of crop disease detection, with YOLO-based models showing promise in addressing the limitations of earlier approaches, particularly in terms of real-time performance and accuracy across various agricultural applications.
Although these improved YOLO models have enhanced the accuracy of crop pest and disease detection, the issue of significant scale variations in crop disease objects has not been adequately addressed. Accurate detection of these targets depends not only on their appearance but also on their contextual semantic information. To address the recognition challenges posed by varying object sizes, some methods have employed data augmentation techniques [ 33 , 34 ] to improve the stability of feature capture across scale variations. Others have adopted dilated convolutions to increase the spatial receptive field, aiming to capture more semantic information. However, it is important to note that using large kernel convolutions [ 35 , 36 ] may introduce substantial background noise, potentially hindering the detection of small disease targets. Moreover, while expanding the receptive field, this approach might overlook fine-grained feature details, leading to feature sparsity. To tackle the challenges of varying scales in crop disease objects, we propose a novel real-time detection for the detection of diseased tomato leaves. Our main contributions are as follows:
(1) We propose a Multi-kernel Inception Module (MKIM) that utilizes a set of parallel multi-kernel depth-wise convolutions to perceive features of targets at different scales. Simultaneously, it employs residual connections and 1 × 1 convolutions to effectively preserve original information and efficiently adjust the number of channels, enhancing the model’s feature fusion and representation capabilities.
(2) Leveraging the proposed MKIM module, we develop MIADN, a Multi-kernel Inception Aggregation Diffusion Network. MIADN is designed to achieve aggregation and diffusion of cross-scale, multi-level, and multi-scale features. This approach aims to extract and fuse contextual semantic information as comprehensively as possible, thereby enhancing the model’s semantic expression capabilities. By providing richer feature details for the detection phase, MIADN significantly improves the accuracy of disease detection.
(3) We incorporate the simple and efficient FasterNet as the backbone for the feature extraction stage. While maintaining low computational redundancy, this approach enhances the ability to extract complex features, further improving the model’s detection accuracy.
(4) Through numerous experiments, we demonstrate the excellent performance of this new real-time detection model for tomato leaf diseases. Compared with the baseline model, our model improves detection accuracy by 4.1%, while surpassing the advanced YOLOv9s by 2.0%. We anticipate that these innovations in tomato disease detection will contribute to advancing research in vegetables pests and diseases detection field.
Datasets : We evaluated our real-time tomato leaf disease detection model on two datasets: the COCO 2017 [ 37 ] object detection dataset and a specialized tomato leaf disease dataset. The tomato leaf disease dataset consists of 4,510 sample images distributed across five categories: 910 healthy leaves and 900 septoria leaf spot collected by us; 900 leaf curl, 900 leaf verticillium wilt, and 900 leaf blight specimens published by CCMT [ 38 ]. To ensure the efficacy of our experiments, we initially annotated the 4,510 images in the tomato leaf disease dataset using the LabelImg annotation tool, generating corresponding XML files with bounding box coordinates and class labels. Subsequently, we partitioned the annotated dataset into training, validation, and test sets using an 8:1:1 ratio. The resulting distribution yielded 3,608 samples for the training set, with 451 samples each allocated to the validation and test sets. The models proposed in the study use PyTorch as the learning framework, and the experimental acceleration is configured with 32G AMD Ryzen 9 7950 × 3D 16-Core Processor 4.20 GHz and NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090. During the training process, 64 × 64 images are randomly cropped from the sample images. The batch size for each GPU is set to 12. All models are trained for 130 cycles, and the models are trained using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.01.

In (a) stage, the input image with dimensions H×W×3 is fed into the FasterNet backbone network [ 39 ]. Through a series of Merging and FasterNet blocks, the resolution of the original image is progressively reduced, generating feature maps at 1/8, 1/16, and 1/32 scales of the original dimensions.
In (b) stage, we propose a multi-scale feature information fusion method called MIADN, as illustrated in Fig. 4 . In the left-to-right pathway, each stage applies identical feature Aggregation and Diffusion operations to the input multi-scale feature information from the preceding stage. This process aims to extract and fuse contextual semantic information as comprehensively as possible, enhancing the semantic expression capability of the MIADN network and providing richer feature details for the detection stage. In the first stage, a customized Feature Aggregation Diffusion network accepts three-scale feature inputs (P3, P4, and P5) obtained from the Feature Extraction Stage. This network incorporates a Multi-kernel Inception Module (MKIM), which utilizes a series of parallel depth-wise convolutions to aggregate information across multiple scales. The MKIM module branches into three paths: (1) One path undergoes upsampling and fusion with the previous P3 scale, followed by a RepNCSPELAN4 [ 40 ] module for further feature extraction and fusion, providing input of the same scale as P3 for the next stage; (2) Another path applies a 3 × 3 standard convolution for downsampling, fuses with the P5 scale, and then passes through an identical RepNCSPELAN4 module, providing input of the same scale as P5 for the next stage. (3) The third path pathway generates features from the MKIM module at a scale equivalent to P3, which are then directly utilized as input for the subsequent stage. Concurrently, the upsampled and downsampled features from the previous stage also serve as inputs for the subsequent stage, as indicated by the Concat sections in Fig. 4 . Finally, the residual link is added, aimed at summing the initial three scale features P3, P4, and P5 with each of the last three output features of the stage, which are then fed into (c) stage, conveying more information about the original features. The diffusion mechanism provides inputs of different scales with rich contextual information to the Object Detection Output Stage, further enhancing the network’s modeling capacity and the model’s expressive power.
Finally, as depicted in Fig. 4 (c), the three branch feature maps are processed in (c) stage using the Decoupled-Head [ 41 ]. This component predicts the boundaries and corresponding categories of tomato disease leaves based on features at three scales, facilitating the computation of their respective losses.
To provide the MIADN network in the feature processing stage with richer feature information, we have selected the efficient FasterNet [ 39 ] as the backbone network for our real-time tomato leaf disease detection model. The overall architecture of the feature extraction stage is illustrated in Fig. 4 (a) stage. The backbone is divided into four stages, each preceded by either an embedding layer or a merging layer. The embedding layer consists of a conv4 × 4 with a stride of 4, while the merging layer is composed of a conv2 × 2 with a stride of 2. These layers primarily serve for spatial downsampling and channel number adjustment. Following the embedding or merging layer, each stage processes the features through a stack of FasterNet blocks. As depicted in Fig. 5 (a), which illustrates the structure of a FasterNet block, two branches are present within each block: (1) The first branch undergoes a PConv operation followed by two Conv 1 × 1 operations (inverted residual block). PConv, a partial convolution operation, reduces redundant computations and content access frequency, thereby more effectively extracting spatial features of the target [ 39 ]. (2) The second branch acts as a residual connection, preserving the diversity of target features. This backbone network design maximizes the utilization of all channel information, increasing the depth and receptive field of the backbone network. Consequently, it enables the extraction of rich feature information from diseased tomato leaves.
This novel real-time recognition model addresses the challenge of precisely identifying various tomato leaf diseases across different scales, surpassing conventional object detection approaches. To tackle this issue, we propose the Multi-kernel Inception (MKIM) module, designed to capture multi-scale texture features effectively. As depicted in Fig. 5 (b), the MKIM module initially processes input features at three distinct scales through upsampling, downsampling, and 1 × 1 convolutions before merging them. Subsequently, the architecture employs a series of parallel depth-wise convolution operations, each incorporating residual connections and varying kernel sizes with unique receptive fields. This parallel configuration is strategically engineered to extract and synthesize contextual feature information across a broad spectrum of spatial scales. Extensive ablation studies informed the selection of 3 × 3, 5 × 5, 7 × 7, and 9 × 9 kernel sizes for the parallel depth-wise convolutions. While fixed convolution kernels may result in the loss of certain feature information when detecting objects at different scales, the multi-kernel parallel depth-wise convolutions facilitate the capture of a more comprehensive range of feature information. The architecture then employs 1 × 1 convolution layers to fuse features from various scales. Concurrently, the five residual connections add together the input features of the previous layers with the processed features, preserving the original feature information while introducing new multiscale information. This approach enhances the model’s ability to retain crucial information while incorporating novel multi-scale insights. In the final stage, the features processed by the 1 × 1 convolution are combined with the initial input features, further augmenting the model’s expressive capacity. In the aforementioned equations, True Positive (TP) denotes the number of disease instances correctly identified by the algorithm. False Positive (FP) represents the number of non-existent disease instances erroneously labeled as positive by the algorithm. False Negative (FN) indicates the number of actual disease instances that the algorithm failed to detect. K represents the total number of object classes in the sample set. The mAP is calculated as the average of the precision values across all object classes, serving as a comprehensive metric for assessing the model’s performance across diverse categories. The precision for each individual class is represented by its corresponding Average Precision (AP) value.

To validate the efficacy of our proposed modules and investigate potential interactions between module combinations, we conducted extensive ablation experiments. As illustrated in Table 3 , we incrementally incorporated each module into the Baseline model, observing the changes in mAP50 and mAP50-95 values. Initially, we observed that the addition of each individual module resulted in performance improvements of 1.8%, 1.7%, and 1.3% in mAP50, respectively. Subsequently, we incorporated pairwise combinations of modules into the Baseline model, which led to further performance enhancements. Compared to the Baseline model, this new architecture demonstrated significant improvements, with mAP50 increasing by 4.1% and mAP50-95 by 6.7%. Ablation of the setting of kernel size in MKIM
Table 4 examines the impact of various parallel combinations of depth-wise convolution kernels on the model’s network performance. The results indicate that the extraction of texture feature information is limited. Employing small single kernels such as 3 × 3, 5 × 5, 7 × 7, or 9 × 9 alone yields suboptimal performance. Conversely, while using large single kernels like 11 × 11 or 13 × 13 does improve performance, it simultaneously increases the model’s Parameters and GFLOPs, potentially introducing more background noise. Our observations reveal that the model achieves peak mAP50 and mAP50-95 values when utilizing depth-wise convolution kernels of 3 × 3, 5 × 5, 7 × 7, and 9 × 9. Based on these findings, we ultimately decided to adopt the combination of 3, 5, 7, and 9 as the depth-wise convolution kernel configuration for the MKIM module.

Study for different sets of lr0 and lrf in our model : Table 5 shows the impact of different sets of lr0 and lrf. In our model, two main hyperparameters are set: the initial learning rate (lr0) and the final learning rate (lrf). lr0 is the learning rate of the optimizer at the beginning of the training, which determines the size of the step size of each parameter update. lrf denotes the ratio of the learning rate at the end of the training to the initial learning rate, which can also be interpreted as the rate of decay of the learning rate. These two hyperparameters are too small to lead to model underfitting, and too large may lead to overfitting. From the six sets of comparison experiments, it can be seen that when both lr0 and lrf are set to 0.01, it is more reasonable and the model performance is optimized.
To visually demonstrate the advantages of our proposed new model in tomato leaf disease detection, we compared it not only with the baseline model but also with the YOLOv5s model [ 45 ], which showed the most significant difference in detection results. Figure 7 provides a visual comparison of the detection results from these three models. As evident from Fig. 7 , lower-performing models exhibit issues such as disease misdetection and inappropriate bounding box sizes. In contrast, the incorporation of the new backbone network, Fasternet, enhances the model’s information extraction capabilities. Simultaneously, the integration of MIADN improves the model’s ability to detect leaves of various sizes. The second set of images, depicting the detection results for leaf spot disease, illustrates that our new model achieves a high detection precision with an mAP50 of 96%, while the other two models reach maximum mAP50 values of only 76% and 92%, respectively. These results demonstrate that our proposed new model exhibits superior performance in tomato leaf disease detection. Consequently, it presents a potential solution for disease target detection in vegetable cultivation processes.

In this study, we have developed a new high-precision real-time detection model for tomato leaf diseases. The model incorporates our newly proposed Multi-kernel Inception Module (MKIM) and Multi-kernel Inception Aggregation Diffusion Network (MIADN), designed to address the challenges posed by variations in tomato disease leaf scales. The MKIM employs parallel depth-wise convolution kernels of different sizes to effectively perceive contextual information, while MIADN efficiently aggregates and diffuses multi-scale features. Additionally, we integrated the simple yet efficient FasterNet into the model’s feature extraction stage, enhancing its ability to extract diverse features. Through extensive experimentation, we have demonstrated that our proposed model achieves state-of-the-art performance on both the COCO dataset and the tomato disease dataset, surpassing current mainstream object detection models.
While our model has made significant progress in tomato leaf disease detection, the transition from experimental results to practical application requires further research support. In subsequent studies, we plan to collect and establish more comprehensive datasets to train and improve model performance. We will also continue to innovate network modules for model lightweight optimization. Future research will focus on integrating lightweight detection models with unmanned aerial vehicles and robots, accelerating the establishment of early prediction and prevention systems for precision vegetable cultivation.
