TITLE: Metabarcoding Profiling Reveals Microbiome Structure and Predicts Functional Shifts in Grapevines Challenged byPhyllosticta ampelicida
URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12597850/

Black rot disease (BRD), caused by the still understudied Phyllosticta ampelicida , is spreading across several grape producing countries, posing a growing threat to the agroindustry. The role of the grapevine microbiome in defending against this pathogen, particularly in terms of microbiota structure and community homeostasis, remains unclear. In this study, we aimed to characterize the epiphytic phyllosphere microbiota of grapevines and identify shifts in microbial genetic structure associated with BRD symptoms. We sampled three vineyards of the cultivar “Touriga Nacional” in the Douro region (Portugal), collecting 20 leaves from (a) five healthy and (b) five BRD-symptomatic grapevines. The presence of P. ampelicida was confirmed in all symptomatic samples. Epiphytic bacterial DNA was extracted and sequenced using next-generation sequencing (NGS). Results indicate that although overall the diversity and richness indexes were not different in diseased plants compared to healthy ones, there was a reduction in OTU richness in black rot-affected grapevines. Diseased plants exhibited significant shifts in microbial network assemblages and showed an increased relative abundance of certain taxa, such as Acinetobacter , suggesting a possible recruitment of beneficial bacteria in response to biotic stress. Additionally, we observed a higher abundance of antibiotic resistance-related KEGG Orthologues (KOS) in symptomatic plants, raising potential concerns for human health. This study presents the first characterization of the grapevine phyllosphere epiphytic bacterial microbiota and its structural shifts in response to BRD.

Vineyards play a keystone role in the landscape, socio-cultural legacy, and economy of many regions, particularly in Europe (e.g., Spain, France, Italy, Portugal), which accounts for approximately 50% of the world’s grape production area, as well as in countries such as China, Turkey, Chile, and South Africa [ 1 ]. However, grapevine health and productivity are constantly challenged by several fungal diseases, including powdery mildew, trunk diseases, gray mold, and black rot [ 2 ], which severely compromise vineyard productivity and longevity, resulting in substantial economic losses [ 3 , 4 ].
Among these, black rot is caused by Phyllosticta ampelicida (asexual morph of Guignardia bidwellii ), a hemibiotrophic ascomycete that infects the plant’s expanding green tissues. The fungus penetrates the host by conidia being attached to the grapevine surface, after which the appressoria is formed. This process allows the fungus to bypass the host’s surface defenses and facilitates penetration in the epidermal cells. After entering the plant tissues, P. ampelicida synthetizes a myriad of carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) to degrade cell wall polysaccharides [ 5 ]. As hyphal elongation proceeds, symptoms begin to emerge as circular lesions with a light-brown center and a dark brown margin, followed by the development of pycnidia. On petioles, elongated dark lesions also bear pycnidia. Infected grapes initially develop light-brown, sunken spots, and within 3–4 days, the fruit becomes mummified, turning bluish-brown and showing the presence of pycnidia [ 6 ]. Current management strategies for black rot include the use of copper- or sulfur-based fungicides. However, in organic farming systems, where conventional fungicides are controlled, available ecological alternatives have so far have proven ineffective at controlling P. ampelicida spread [ 7 ].
The susceptibility to BRD varies among grapevine cultivars [ 5 ], although all commercially relevant cultivars are considered susceptible.
Despite its growing prevalence, little is known about how P. ampelicida influences grapevine associated microbial communities, particularly epiphytic ones. Recent studies suggest that plant health is not only shaped by pathogens but also by the host microbiome, which can facilitate nutrient uptake, enhancing tolerance to abiotic and biotic stress, and contributing to disease suppression [ 8 , 9 ]. These communities are highly dynamic and can respond rapidly to pathogen attack and changes in the microclimate. Therefore, the function of a holobiont cannot be inferred solely from the presence or absence of individual microbial taxa [ 10 ].
In this context, while the rhizosphere has been extensively studied [ 11 ], the phyllosphere has received little attention, despite its direct interface with the aerial environment and its potential impact on plant health at the community level [ 12 ]. Notably, recent research suggests that phyllosphere microbial communities may enhance host resistance to foliar pathogens [ 13 ]. Moreover, pathogen attacks can induce significant shifts in the plant-associated microbiome, including the phyllosphere, altering both its taxonomic composition and functional potential [ 14 ]. This is particularly important regarding the epiphytic microbial community since it constitutes the first line of defense against foliar pathogens. Epiphytic microbiota can influence the pathogens’ ability to establish in the plant surface and effectively colonize the tissues, playing a pivotal role in the early stages of infection [ 15 ]. Furthermore, the status of the epiphytic community has often been regarded as an indicator of plant health since it is highly responsive to environmental conditions and farming practices. Thus, some works have shown that changes in microclimatic conditions can influence the epiphytic microbial community and the host’s susceptibility to pathogens [ 16 , 17 ].
These imbalances in microbial community structure and function, often driven by biotic or abiotic stressors, are collectively referred to as dysbiosis [ 18 ]. In the phyllosphere, dysbiosis has been associated with reduced alpha diversity, a shift from Firmicutes-dominated to Proteobacteria-rich communities, and the development of chlorotic or necrotic lesions in leaf tissue [ 19 ]. Some studies have further reported an increase in the abundance of phyllosphere endophytes in response to pathogen challenge [ 20 ]. Remarkably, transplantation experiments have shown that inoculating healthy plants with dysbiotic phyllosphere communities can induce tissue damage, suggesting that dysbiosis itself can directly compromise host health [ 19 ].
However, microbiome shifts do not always result in negative outcomes. In certain cases, pathogen-induced changes may lead to meliorbiosis, a state in which the host responds positively, often by recruiting beneficial microorganisms that enhance resilience to stress [ 21 , 22 ]. Meliorbiosis is commonly associated with improved resistance to secondary pathogens or enhanced plant vigor, typically through the selective enrichment of protective microbial taxa [ 23 – 25 ]. In support of this, recent studies have highlighted that stressed plants can modify their exudate profiles to selectively recruit beneficial microbes [ 26 ]. Moreover, in the phyllosphere, rice leaves infected by Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae have been reported to recruit beneficial bacteria (e.g., Pantoea ) via metabolic changes, and ultimately suppress the pathogen [ 27 ].
Although plant–microbe interactions have been under intense focus in the last few years, most agriculturally important pathosystems remain poorly understood. In particular, the impact of P. ampelicida infection on the grapevine microbiota is still unknown. It remains to be determined whether the host microbiome faces dysbiosis under pathogen pressure, or whether Vitis vinifera can mount a meliorbiotic response by selectively recruiting beneficial microorganisms.
Therefore, in this study, we hypothesize that infection by P. ampelicida induces a shift in the grapevine phyllosphere microbiota, resulting in either (a) reduced diversity and increased abundance of opportunistic taxa (dysbiosis), or (b) enrichment of specific beneficial microbial taxa associated with disease suppression (meliorbiosis). Thus, we aim to characterize the microbiota of V. vinifera under P. ampelicida challenge, to determine whether the host recruits beneficial microbes to counteract infection or, conversely, enters a dysbiotic state that may increase vulnerability to other pathogens and pests.
Three commercial vineyards used for Douro wine production were selected in the “Baixo Corgo” region, a subregion of the Douro Vinhateiro (Portugal). The selected orchards use integrated farming practices, vertical shoot positioning systems, with Guyot pruning. The orchards periodically apply Bordeaux mixture, Revus® and Manzate® 800, furthermore, grapevines were evenly distributed between rows. In each vineyard, five Vitis vinifera cv. “Touriga Nacional” plants showing no symptoms of black rot and five plants exhibiting clear symptoms of the disease (characterized by leaf lesions bearing pycnidia) were selected. To minimize the likelihood of pathogen presence in asymptomatic plants, at least two buffer rows, free from visible black rot symptoms and from which no plant material was collected, were maintained between asymptomatic and symptomatic sampling areas. From each selected plant, 20 fully expanded leaves (10 from each main branch) were collected, ensuring that the sampled leaves showed no visible symptoms of other pests or diseases. All leaf samples were stored at 4 °C until DNA extraction. After washing (see Sect. below), the leaf pools were powdered in liquid N 2 and stored at −80 °C.
To assess the presence of P. ampelicida , a conventional PCR reaction was performed using the DNA extracted from leaf powder and specific primers. In brief, the PCR mix consisted of 10 µL of NZYTaqII 2 × Green Master Mix (NZYTech, Lisbon, Portugal) were mixed with 2 µL of DNA template and 2 µL of Forward and Reverse primers (Bidwell_F, GAAAAGCCGTCCGAAAGAGCC; Ampel2_R, CAGGACTTCACGAAATAATCG) as described by Moreno-Velázquez et al. [ 28 ]. The final reaction volume was adjusted to 20 µL with 6 µL of Milli-Q water. PCR cycling conditions were set according to Moreno-Velázquez et al. [ 28 ]: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 45 s, annealing at 62 °C for 30 s, and an extension at 72 °C for 30 s. A final extension was performed at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were visualized on a 1% (w/v) agarose gel.
To isolate the epiphytic microbial community, 20 leaves from each plant were pooled and washed with 60 mL of sterile MgSO 4 solution (10 mM) for 2.5 min per side under agitation at 250 rpm, following established protocols [ 29 – 31 ]. The resulting leaf wash solutions were filtered through 0.22 µm polyethene (PE) syringe filters. The filter membranes were then collected and stored at −20 °C until DNA extraction. Following the procedure described by Jiang et al. [ 32 ] for microbial DNA isolation from filter membranes, the E.Z.N.A ® Soil DNA Kit (Omega, Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA) was used to extract the DNA from the filter membranes (epiphytic community) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.
To characterize the microbiota of asymptomatic V. vinifera plants and challenged by P. ampelicida , amplicon metagenomic sequencing of the V3–V4 region of the 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was carried out, at Novogene Europe (Novogene Co., Cambridge, UK), using the primers described by Yu et al. [ 33 ], 341F—CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG and 806R—GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT. Briefly, after passing the quality control (QC), and amplification of the 16S region, library construction was made with the amplicons (one library per sample). Sequencing by synthesis (SBS) technology was used resorting to the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina®, San Diego, CA, USA) to generate 250-bp paired-end raw reads. After sequencing was completed, QC and bioinformatics analysis were conducted.
Metagenomic data analysis was performed using pipelines provided by Novogene. After assessing sequencing coverage and quality, operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were identified using the QIIME2 (v2023.5) [ 34 ] and UPARSE (UPARSE v7.0.1090) [ 35 ] software with a threshold of at least 97% similarity. Sequencing data was normalized by rarefaction, using QIIME2, with a depth of 30,949 reads. Taxonomic annotation of representative tags was conducted using BLASTALL (Version 2.2.25) against the Unite (V8.2) database, allowing the identification of the ten most abundant bacterial phyla and their relative abundances in grapevine leaf samples. Alpha-diversity was assessed for each sample using the Shannon diversity index and Chao1 richness estimator. A t -test was performed to assess differences between the healthy and P. ampelicida -infected plants. Common taxa among both conditions were identified through Venn diagram analysis. Beta-diversity was evaluated via weighted unifrac to explore differences in microbial community structure between samples. Both alpha and beta diversity analyses were carried out in QIIME2 using the Chao1 estimator and Shannon index tools. The SVG function in Perl was used for the Top10 barplot. Other visualizations were generated using R software (Version 2.15.3; R Core Team, 2013), namely for the PCoA, the ade4 and ggplot2 packages were employed, and the Venn diagram function was used to generate the Venn diagram. To analyze differentially abundant OTUs between healthy and infected groups, the package MetagenomeSeq (v 1.16.0) was used [ 36 ]. PICRUSt (Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States) was used to predict the functional capabilities of the microbiota [ 37 ]. A t -test of the log-transformed data was performed to assess the differences in differentially abundant KOs in healthy vs. P. ampelicida -infected grapevine samples.
To construct the co-occurrence network, the Python3 packages Pandas, Numpy, Network, Matplotlib, Networx and Scipy were used. Positive and negative interactions were inferred using Sparse Correlations for Compositional data (SparCC) [ 38 ]. Nodes represent taxa, and edges indicate significant positive (green) or negative (red) correlations. Network construction and analysis were performed using NetworkX in Python. Core taxa were defined based on prevalence and relative abundance: taxa present in more than 50% of samples and those with abundance above the median were considered part of the core microbiota [ 39 ]. Hub taxa were identified based on degree centrality within the co-occurrence network; the top 10% of nodes with the highest degree centrality values were classified as hub taxa [ 40 ]. The modularity analysis was carried out using the NetworX and Python-Louvain packages for Python3.

This study aimed to characterize shifts in the epiphytic leaf-associated bacterial community of grapevines (cv. “Touriga Nacional”) challenged by P. ampelicida and exhibiting BRD symptoms. Our findings reveal that infected leaves undergo notable changes in their epiphytic bacterial community’s structure and interactions. Our data also suggests that several genes associated with antibiotic resistance, virulence mechanisms, and primary metabolic processes are significantly over-abundant in P. ampelicida -challenged grapevines.
Although no significant differences were observed in richness and diversity indices, the epiphytic bacterial community of grapevine leaves challenged by P. ampelicida exhibited a slightly higher relative abundance of Proteobacteria and Cyanobacteria. This pattern, particularly the trend to increase in Proteobacteria, may reflect a shift in microbial community composition, compromising the plant’s natural defense capacities and making it more susceptible to BRD. This alongside a reduction in Actinobacteria, has previously been associated with dysbiosis, as reported in Arabidopsis thaliana infected with Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato [ 19 ]. Moreover, a meta-analysis carried out in various plant systems has shown that reduced relative abundances of Proteobacteria and increased Actinobacteria have been associated with disease suppression capabilities [ 41 ]. Proteobacteria enrichment has been proposed as a general marker of dysbiosis in microbial communities [ 19 ]. In our study, infected grapevine plants also exhibited considerable shifts in microbiome assemblage compared to healthy plants, with only 31.17% of the epiphytic bacterial taxa being shared by both conditions in the Douro Vinhateiro region. This marked reduction in community overlap further supports the hypothesis of pathogen-induced dysbiosis.
We also observed an increased abundance of Cyanobacteria in grapevine leaves challenged by P. ampelicida. Previous studies have reported that Cyanobacteria abundance may rise following pathogen infection, as some members of this phylum possess nitrogen-fixing capabilities that can enhance plant fitness under stress conditions [ 13 , 42 ]. In addition, Cyanobacteria have been identified as promising candidates for the biocontrol of fungal plant diseases, with demonstrated efficacy against pathogens such as Fusarium spp. in tomato crops [ 42 , 43 ]. It should be noted that the role of Cyanobacteria in plant health remains unclear. Further studies are needed to ascertain whether Cyanobacteria could be targeted as biocontrol tools for fungal diseases of grapevine or if they constitute suitable biomarkers for the detection of susceptible microbiota.
On the other hand, several studies have identified the Actinobacteriota phylum as a key contributor to plant defense. Reduced abundance of Actinobacteria has been correlated with increased disease incidence in crops such as tomato [ 44 ]. This observation aligns with our findings, which show that healthy grapevine plants (cv. “Touriga Nacional”) exhibit a higher relative abundance of Actinobacteriota, particularly Leifsonia sp. and members of the Gaiellales order, compared to plants affected by black rot. Previous studies have linked these taxa to host stress responses, including roles in phytohormone production, amino acid transport, and plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) functions, such as nitrogen fixation [ 45 ]. Notably, Gaiellales have been reported to exhibit suppressive activity against potato scab caused by Streptomyces spp. [ 46 ], further supporting their potential role in biocontrol and host resilience.
Myxococcota are widely recognized as keystone taxa in shaping microbial community structure and organization [ 47 ]. Several studies have reported that lower relative abundance of this phylum is associated with increased plant susceptibility to disease [ 48 ]. This may be attributed to their role as microbial predators, capable of suppressing plant pathogens, thereby positioning them as potential biological control agents [ 49 ]. Our findings are consistent with these observations, as we detected a higher abundance of Myxococcota in healthy grapevine plants compared to those infected by P. ampelicida . This suggests that their presence may contribute to enhanced pathogen resistance, potentially through predatory activity with the phyllosphere microbiome. Further works should explore strategies for promoting Myxococcota in the epiphytic leaf microbiome as part of ecological grapevine management practices and disclose their effectiveness in controlling fungal pathogens.
This study also highlights that under P. ampelicida infection, grapevine plants show an increased abundance of potential plant growth promoters such as Azospirillum sp., Exiguobacterium sp., and Acinetobacter sp. (Proteobacteria), Previous research has shown that these genera are involved in inducing the production of polyphenols, which are known to contribute to biotic stress responses, and in activating key antioxidant defense enzymes such as polyphenol oxidase (PPO) and phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) [ 50 ]. Moreover, these taxa have been associated with enhanced lipoxygenase (LOX) activity in grapevine leaves challenged with Botrytis cinerea [ 51 ]. These findings suggest that some bacterial taxa, traditionally recognized for their roles in promoting plant growth through rhizosphere interactions, may also function effectively in the phyllosphere, playing a critical role in the plant’s defense against hemibiotrophic fungal pathogens such as P. ampelicida .
Furthermore, the increased abundance of Exiguobacterium sp. observed in this study aligns with previous findings, where this taxon has been associated with plant stress responses, namely hemibiotroph-induced disease suppression in wheat, particularly in response to Rhizoctonia solani infection [ 52 , 53 ]. Similarly, the role of Acinetobacter spp. in the suppression of fungal diseases in plants has been well documented [ 54 , 55 ], largely due to their production of iturins, secondary metabolites with strong antifungal properties [ 56 ]. This compositional shift may reflect a microbiome-mediated adaptation to biotic stress, potentially contributing to increased host resilience. These shifts in relative abundance of important taxa with PGP and disease suppression properties provide insights into potential targets for future ecological biocontrol strategies, specifically designed for P. ampelicida management.
In addition, analysis of bacterial interactions reveals that P. ampelicida infection induced a substantial shift in the microbial co-occurrence network, marked by an overall reduction in the number of microbial associations. Regarding core composition, data shows that healthy grapevine leaves harbor a diverse and interconnected core microbiota, composed of families like Burkholderiaceae, Sphingomonadaceae, and Streptomycetaceae, which have already been reported as core taxa in plant studies [ 57 ]. Under infection, the core composition shifts to include taxa like Azospirillaceae, Pseudonocardiaceae, Mycobacteriaceae), which have been reported as important taxa in plants under stress conditions [ 58 , 59 ]. This compositional shift, along with increased negative microbial interactions and reduced community overlap, is a recognized signature of pathogen-induced dysbiosis and has been linked to compromised plant defense in multiple systems. Moreover, a pronounced shift in the core microbiota of infected grapevine leaves was observed. Although Actinobacteria still dominates the core microbiome under infection, there is a notable increase in the relative abundance of taxa typically associated with stress-adapted environments, including Pseudonocardiaceae, Mycobacteriaceae, and Kineosporiaceae [ 60 , 61 ]. As for healthy plants, they exhibit a core microbiota. This compositional shift indicates that P. ampelicida disturbs the microbiota of grapevine plants, causing dysbiosis. Moreover, it should be noted that Wicaksono et al. [ 62 ] disclosed the microbiota interactions of several cultivars of grapevine’s phyllosphere and uncovered that Sphingomonadaceae was present as a core taxa, which is similar to our findings for healthy cv. “Touriga Nacional.”
This study also revealed that P. ampelicida infection is associated with an increased abundance of epiphytic bacteria taxa harboring antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), consistent with hypotheses proposed by Li et al. [ 63 ]. The restructuring of the microbial interaction network may have facilitated enhanced connectivity between ARG-bearing taxa, such as Pseudomonadaceae, Bacillaceae, Alicyclobacillaceae, and other families like Acidobacteriaceae (Subgroup 1) and Gemmataceae [ 64 – 66 ]. The biotic stress imposed by P. ampelicida appears to promote the recruitment or enrichment of bacteria with antibiotic resistance traits by the grapevine holobiont. These findings align with existing evidence, which shows that plant-associated resistomes can be internalized and transferred through the food chain, a topic of interest under the One Health research framework [ 67 ].
This study provides the first characterization of shifts in the grapevine phyllosphere microbiome triggered by P. ampelicida infection. Our findings reveal that, under fungal attack, grapevine plants may recruit beneficial microbial taxa, potentially as part of a meliorbiotic response, to help mitigate pathogen impact. In contrast, healthy plants maintain a microbiome enriched in taxa known for their biocontrol potential. Moreover, we observed a higher abundance of antibiotic resistance KOs in infected plants, raising new concerns from a public human health perspective. These findings highlight the need for further investigation into the ecological consequences of pathogen-driven microbiome restructuring in agroecosystems and its potential implications across the plant–soil–human health continuum.
