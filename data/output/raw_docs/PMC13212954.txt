TITLE: Robust apple leaf disease diagnosis for sustainable horticulture: overcoming background noise with a multilayer transformer-based approach
URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC13212954/

Apples are one of the most economically significant and widely cultivated fruit crops worldwide, contributing substantially to food security and the horticultural economy. However, their production is frequently compromised by diseases such as Alternaria leaf spot, Apple Mosaic, Powdery Mildew, and Apple Scab, leading to significant yield losses and increased dependence on chemical control. Timely and accurate disease diagnosis is critical to minimize crop damage, reduce pesticide usage, and promote sustainable horticultural practices. This study proposes the Multilayer Transformer-based Apple Disease Classification (MTADC) model, an advanced deep learning framework designed for early and robust identification of apple leaf diseases. MTADC employs a two-stage learning approach: global feature extraction using a transformer encoder, followed by class-specific mapping through a refined classification head. By incorporating a self-attention mechanism, the model effectively suppresses background noise and enhances feature discrimination, even under variable field conditions. Unlike existing models that require well-constrained, high-quality images captured under ideal lighting and angles, MTADC is designed for deployment in real-world field conditions, allowing disease detection from diverse and unconstrained field images commonly captured by farmers. Experiments on a curated dataset comprising publicly available and field-acquired images demonstrate that MTADC achieves a classification accuracy of 96.3%, outperforming conventional convolutional models. These results highlight the model’s robustness, scalability, and potential to be an accessible tool for digital plant health monitoring and precision horticulture.
Apples (Malus spp.) are a cornerstone of global horticulture and one of the most widely cultivated and consumed fruit crops worldwide. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), global apple production has reached approximately 90 million metric tons in recent years, with China, the United States, Poland, and India being major producers 1 , 2 . Apples play a vital role in food security and human nutrition due to their rich content of vitamins, minerals, and antioxidants, which contribute to the prevention of cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and cancer. Beyond their nutritional significance, apples are economically important in temperate regions, supporting rural livelihoods and contributing billions of dollars annually to global trade 3 , 4 . Although apples can grow in a range of soil types, they prefer well-drained, fertile loamy soils. However, apple productivity is severely threatened by various diseases that reduce yield and fruit quality, thereby affecting marketability and profitability. Alternaria leaf spot, Apple Mosaic, Powdery Mildew, and Apple Scab are among the major diseases affecting apple crops (Fig. 1 ), and these are discussed in detail below:

The United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) estimates that to fulfil future food demands, agricultural production must be increased by 70% by 2050. Studies indicate that plant diseases account for approximately 26% of primary yield loss and 38% of secondary yield loss globally, posing a significant challenge to sustainable agricultural practices 5 . In the case of apples, the effective identification and management of the diseases are therefore critical to ensuring high-quality apple production and meeting the growing global demand for this nutrient-dense fruit. Early disease identification and natural treatment are essential components of effective disease management. Appropriate action cannot be taken without accurate identification of apple leaf diseases. Moreover, late identification of these diseases often necessitates increased use of pesticides, posing greater risks to both the environment and human health. Early detection and classification of these diseases are essential to minimize losses, reduce the frequency of chemical sprays, and ensure sustainable production. The traditional approach to managing apple diseases relies majorly on manual inspection and applying chemical pesticides. While these methods have been somewhat effective, they are neither sustainable nor precise. Manual inspection is labour-intensive, time-consuming, and often prone to errors, especially over large orchards. On the other hand, excessive and indiscriminate use of chemical pesticides poses serious environmental and human health risks, including soil degradation, water contamination, and the emergence of pesticide-resistant pathogens.
Recent advancements in machine learning and computer vision have opened new avenues for addressing these challenges. Automated disease detection systems offer a promising alternative by enabling rapid, accurate, and scalable identification of plant diseases. However, implementing machine learning algorithms in agriculture (especially for disease detection and classification) in many countries worldwide has yet to receive considerable attention. Identification of plant diseases is a crucial and practical issue in agriculture because, during the growth phase of a plant, it shows many forms, and it becomes quite challenging to understand the symptoms of a disease. With this aim, researchers are trying to develop different machine learning-based models for detecting and identifying crop diseases in their early stages. However, using advanced deep learning-based models for crop disease (especially for apple crops) identification is still missing in the literature 6 – 9 . This motivated us to develop a novel deep learning-based model that can accurately detect and classify apple crop diseases in their early stages. Moreover, the great success of the vision transformer for image analysis task 10 also motivated us to develop a new Multilayer Transformer based Apple Disease Classifier (MTADC). The proposed MTADC offers unparalleled crop disease detection and classification capabilities by harnessing the power of advanced image analysis techniques. The proposed model allows individuals from diverse backgrounds to leverage its capabilities within the agriculture field. The major limitation of the current methodologies is that they require a properly constrained image of the leaves for accurate classification. Current models are pretty sensitive to background noises and require specific lighting and angles. However, in the field, it is not feasible to capture such high-quality images by the farmers. Essentially, the focus is on designing a model that doesn’t require stringent environmental conditions or specialized expertise to operate effectively. This work aims to detect and classify apple diseases, focusing on four prevalent diseases: Apple Mosaic, Powdery Mildew, and Apple Scab, using the proposed MTADC model.

A domain-specific multilayer Transformer architecture (MTADC) is proposed for apple leaf disease classification under unconstrained field conditions. Unlike standard Vision Transformer and hybrid CNN–Transformer frameworks, the proposed architecture directly models global contextual dependencies using multilayer self-attention, improving robustness to background noise and illumination variability. A lightweight incremental positional encoding-based Patch Encoder module is introduced to enhance computational efficiency for limited agricultural datasets. The proposed encoding strategy reduces patch interaction complexity and enables stable learning without requiring large-scale pretraining, making it suitable for resource-constrained agricultural applications. A multilayer attention learning strategy is developed to extract discriminative disease-specific features while suppressing irrelevant background information. This hierarchical attention mechanism improves feature–disease mapping and enhances classification accuracy in noisy field environments. Since the introduction of the deep convolutional network of AlexNet by Krizhevsky et al., 11 , deep learning has made an unprecedented breakthrough in the development of computer vision applications. It has also been widely used in identifying and diagnosing plant diseases due to its better extraction and robustness. Recent literature in disease classification showcases advancements in machine learning techniques such as Transformer, CNN, and Ensemble Learning methods for accurate and automated diagnosis 12 , 13 . A discussion of popular deep learning-based crop disease detection and classification is presented below.
DenseNet-121 model 14 with multi-label classification and improved regression method was proposed by Zhong and Zhao 15 for Apple leaf disease recognition. This model used a focus loss function to identify apple leaf diseases. This achieved 93.51% accuracy on the test set, which was better than the traditional multi-classification method 14 based on cross-entropy loss function with an accuracy of 92.29% using only DenseNet. This research also demonstrated a dynamic way for faster convergence of the model. To speed up model convergence, this article used the pre-training weights of DenseNet 121.
Jiang 16 proposed a model for the recognition of four rice leaf diseases based on CNN and support vector machine (SVM). In this, CNN is used to extract the rice leaf disease features the SVM follows to classify and predict the specific disease. The optimal parameters of SVM are obtained through the 10-fold cross-validation approach. This paper emphasizes that spot color is one of the important criteria for agricultural experts to identify rice diseases 17 . Therefore, in this model image colors have been used as the distinguishing feature. They used a more intuitive way to separate colours provided by Yang et al. 18 . They extracted the colour feature of lesion images in RGB, HSI, and YCbCr colour space to distinguish the colour feature. The experimental results show that this work obtained an accuracy of 96.8% for different rice disease classifications which is higher than that of the traditional back propagation neural networks models. The limitation of this work is that the model required noise-free images which makes it impractical for real world images.
An alternate study by Rao et al. 19 aimed to detect and classify Grape and Mango leaf diseases, employing a dataset of 8438 images of diseased and healthy leaves collected from the Plant Village dataset. This study focused on the utilization of transfer learning of pre-existing models. Kunduracioglu 20 utilized ResNet Architectures for Identification of Tomato Diseases, and Kunduracioglu and Pacal 21 used EfficientNet models for disease detection in Sugarcane leaves. A pre-trained CNN architecture called AlexNet 11 is employed for automatic feature extraction and classification, and the refined deep CNN is trained to identify diseases. This model is trained to first classify the input as healthy or unhealthy leaves and then further classify the type of disease in unhealthy leaves. This model achieved an average accuracy of 94%, with the accuracy range varying across classes from 89% (in Mango’s class) to 99% (in Grape’s class). However, this is clear that this approach is not suitable in the general scenario as without additional tuning, the accuracy will vary vastly across multiple classes.
Further, another work by Rangarajan et al. 22 was proposed using transfer learning with slight customization. Here, the Visual Geometry Group (VGG-16) model 23 was taken as a base model and customized some hyperparameters for fine-tuning the model for their dataset. VGG16 with pre-trained parameters was used for the experiment. The last fully connected layer of the VGG16 had been modified according to the number of disease classes. In this case, the number of neurons in the last fully connected was modified to five which is the number of disease classes. There are several hyperparameters that have been fine-tuned to obtain a trained model that performs better for the disease classification. It produced an accuracy of 94.3% for different brinjal disease classifications. The accuracy of this model fell significantly when images had higher incident angles of sunlight.
After this, the improved ResNet Based Apple Leaf Diseases Identification model was proposed 24 . This work used the ResNet18 25 classification network as a baseline. As the symptomatic area of some leaf diseases can be small, a Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) 26 was considered to improve the feature representation ability. The CBAM is a lightweight attention module that can be embedded into CNN, and introduces approximately negligible amount of computation and parameters. It can improve the recognition ability of the apple leaf disease classification model. Their ResNet-CBAM model achieved an accuracy of 95.2% on a dataset crafted within a controlled scientific environment. However, in a real-world scenario, farmers cannot produce images of such quality, limiting the above model’s performance.
Another generalized model proposed by Paymode and Malode 27 , utilized the VGG model 23 to detect the grapes and tomato leaves disease in the early stage. This method also utilized the concept of multi-class labeling, where it first classified the leaves as healthy or diseased. Then within the diseased class, it identified the particular disease. The pre-trained weights of the VGG model are used to train the model which achieved a varying range of accuracy from 95.71% for tomatoes and 98.4% for grapes. This method indeed offers a versatile model applicable across multiple crops. Nevertheless, its accuracy is significantly hampered by the pervasive issue of noisy datasets. This compels a reliance on manual intervention or other suitable techniques to effectively eliminate noise before inputting the image into the model. This step becomes pivotal in ensuring the model’s accuracy and reliability in real-world applications.
A distinct report by Zhang et al. 8 , 9 used a pre-trained Siamese dilated Inception network (SDINet) 28 as a base network. The dilated Inception module is introduced into AlexNet 11 to construct two subnetworks for SDINet, and two subnetworks are responsible for extracting multi-scale features from image pairs in the same layer. The global pooling instead of the fully connected layers is utilized to reduce the number of model parameters without any feature loss. SDINet is trained with image pairs, each consisting of two real diseased leaf images or one real disease and one healthy leaf image. The SDINet makes full use of the advantages of multi-scale dilated Inception to enrich and improve the information and enhance the adaptability of the model. Unlike the existing deep CNNs, SDINet used cosine distance learning to calculate the similarity between the leaf image pairs to recognize apple diseases. This model can work efficiently with few training samples and is suitable for smaller datasets. This model achieved an accuracy of 94.17% without needing a large dataset. However, the performance of this model in real-world scenarios is not devoid of degradation due to environmental factors. This model is incapable of extracting the features from those images which are captured under an uncontrolled environment.
Recent advances in computer vision have demonstrated the effectiveness of Transformer-based architectures for image classification tasks, including plant disease detection. Vision Transformer (ViT) models utilize self-attention mechanisms to capture long-range dependencies and global contextual information, which is particularly advantageous for complex visual scenes in agricultural environments 10 . Several studies have reported that Transformer-based models exhibit improved robustness to illumination variations, background clutter, and occlusions in agricultural image analysis.
Hybrid CNN–Transformer architectures have also been developed to combine the strong local feature extraction capability of convolutional neural networks with the global contextual modeling ability of Transformers. Architectures such as Swin Transformer 29 , ConvNeXt 30 , and DeiT 31 have achieved competitive performance in plant disease recognition tasks, while EfficientNet-Transformer variants have demonstrated improved computational efficiency.
Compared with these general-purpose architectures, the proposed Multilayer Transformer-based Apple Disease Classification (MTADC) model is specifically tailored for field-level apple disease detection under unconstrained imaging conditions. Unlike conventional ViT frameworks that require large-scale pretraining and computationally intensive positional encoding strategies, MTADC incorporates a lightweight incremental positional encoding mechanism and multilayer attention learning designed for limited agricultural datasets. Furthermore, while hybrid CNN–Transformer models exploit convolutional inductive biases for local feature extraction, MTADC focuses on hierarchical self-attention to capture global contextual dependencies relevant to complex orchard environments. The model is trained on a hybrid dataset comprising field-acquired and public images, enhancing robustness to background noise and illumination variability.
Deployment of deep learning models in real-world agricultural environments often leads to performance degradation due to uncontrolled imaging conditions, including background clutter, illumination variability, occlusions, and sensor noise. Training models under unconstrained field conditions is therefore essential for practical disease diagnosis. Zhang et al. 8 , 9 proposed a multi-scale deep learning framework for apple leaf spot detection in unconstrained environments, achieving an accuracy of 85.23%, demonstrating the feasibility of field-deployable disease detection systems. Recent studies have also explored various apple disease detection approaches, including the Simple Linear Iterative Clustering combined with the Kennard–Stone algorithm for apple mosaic disease detection 6 , 7 , Siamese dilated Inception networks for apple disease recognition 8 , 9 , and multi-scale constrained deformable convolutional networks for apple leaf disease classification 6 , 7 . These models have shown promising performance under controlled experimental settings; however, their robustness under real-world field conditions remains limited.
Consequently, there is a growing need for domain-specific Transformer-based frameworks capable of handling complex background noise and illumination variability encountered in practical horticultural settings. The proposed MTADC model is designed to address these challenges through multilayer self-attention mechanisms and domain-adaptive training on mixed field and public datasets.
The proposed MTADC framework is inspired by the Vision Transformer (ViT) architecture; however, it is not a direct implementation of ViT. MTADC incorporates domain-specific architectural adaptations, including incremental positional encoding, hierarchical feature aggregation across transformer layers, and a deployment-oriented optimization pipeline. These modifications are designed to improve robustness for real-field agricultural images characterized by background noise, illumination variability, and class imbalance. Therefore, MTADC should be viewed as a domain-adapted transformer framework rather than a generic ViT model. The detailed step by step working of the proposed MTADC model is shown in Fig. 2 . As can be seen from Fig. 2 , the proposed model begins by taking an image as an input, which is then divided into multiple patches. These patches are flattened into one-dimensional vectors, converting each 2D patch into a single row of pixel values. Next, each vector undergoes a linear transformation, which maps it to a higher dimensional space. This increases the number of elements in each vector, allowing the model to capture more detailed and complex information from the patches, making them ready for further processing. After that, positional encoding is applied to each patch.
This encoding embeds spatial information into the patches, which helps the model to understand their relative positions within the image. Next, the patches and their positional encodings are passed into the Transformer Block. Within the transformer block, attention scores are calculated and applied 32 . These attention scores allow the model to focus on relevant patches and capture global dependencies. This process is repeated for each layer of the transformer block, as specified. Finally, after multiple iterations through the transformer block, the features learned by the MTADC model are aggregated and refined. These features are then mapped to their respective classes using dense layers. The dense layers act as a classifier, interpreting the learned features and assigning probabilities to each class, ultimately determining the most likely class for an input image. The primary goal of the proposed model is to classify an image, not to convert the image into any other form, which can be implemented through the encoder part only. Hence, the multilayer transformer of the proposed model does not have a decoder part.
The proposed MTADC model is a multilayer transformer model in which attention scores are calculated at multiple levels. It has multiple sub-modules that work together to accomplish the task (Fig. 3 ). To ensure clarity regarding the interconnection between modules, state notation (i.e., S1, S2, ..., S5) is utilized to depict that the output of each module, denoted by a specific state, is exclusively forwarded to the subsequent module in the system. The Patch Encoder module (Fig. 4 ) is designed to generate image patches and incorporate positional information into each patch representation. The input image is first partitioned into non-overlapping patches to enable the transformer model to learn spatial relationships and inter-patch dependencies. In this study, the input image of size 256 × 256 is divided into 256 patches of size 16 × 16. This patch size provides an appropriate balance between spatial resolution and computational efficiency. Since transformer architectures are permutation invariant, positional information must be injected into the patch embeddings to preserve spatial structure. Therefore, positional encoding is added to each patch embedding to provide spatial context 33 . For apple disease classification experiments, a hybrid dataset consisting of field-acquired and publicly available images was curated. Field images were collected from the experimental apple orchard of ICAR–Central Institute of Temperate Horticulture (ICAR-CITH), Srinagar, Jammu and Kashmir, India (Latitude: 33.98° N; Longitude: 74.80° E), under unconstrained field conditions, including natural illumination variations, occlusions, and background clutter. These field-acquired images correspond to three disease categories: Alternaria, Apple Mosaic Virus, and Powdery Mildew. To increase dataset diversity and include an additional disease class, publicly available Apple Scab images were obtained from a Kaggle repository, consisting of 4748 images. After quality filtering and preprocessing, the final dataset comprised 4748 images across four disease classes: 274 Alternaria, 208 Mosaic, 180 Powdery Mildew, and 4086 Scab images (Fig. 1 ). The dataset is inherently imbalanced, reflecting realistic field conditions where disease incidence varies across classes. Specifically, field-acquired images constituted 662 samples (Alternaria, Mosaic, and Powdery Mildew), while publicly sourced images accounted for 4086 samples (Apple Scab). This strategic combination of field and public datasets enhances variability in imaging conditions and enables evaluation of the model’s robustness under both controlled and real-world scenarios. Class imbalance was retained intentionally to evaluate the ability of the proposed MTADC model to generalize under skewed class distributions commonly encountered in practical horticultural monitoring applications.
To ensure uniformity and improve model convergence, all images were resized to a fixed resolution of 256 × 256 pixels. This resolution provides a balance between computational efficiency and preservation of disease-related visual features. Pixel intensities were normalized to the range [0, 1] by dividing by 255 to stabilize gradient-based optimization and accelerate training convergence. To prevent data leakage, the dataset was first split into training and testing subsets using an 80:20 stratified ratio. The training subset was further divided into training and validation sets in a 70:30 ratio. Data augmentation was applied only to the training set to increase sample diversity and mitigate overfitting. Augmentation operations included random rotations within ± 60° and shearing transformations with a magnitude range of 0 to 0.2. Through augmentation, the effective training dataset size was increased fourfold, resulting in 18,992 training samples. Given the inherent class imbalance in the dataset, no oversampling was applied to preserve the real-world disease distribution. Instead, the robustness of the proposed MTADC model under skewed class distributions was evaluated using multiple performance metrics, including accuracy, precision and F1-score. This approach reflects realistic orchard disease prevalence and evaluates model generalization in practical horticultural scenarios.

The F1score is a metric commonly used in machine learning to evaluate a model’s performance, especially in binary classification tasks. It combines precision and recall into a single value, providing a balanced measure that considers both false positives and false negatives. A high F1 score indicates that the model has a good balance between precision (the proportion of true positive predictions among all positive predictions) and recall (the proportion of true positive predictions among all actual positive instances). The F1 score is particularly useful when dealing with imbalanced datasets, where one class dominates the data, as it takes into account the model’s ability to correctly identify both positive and negative instances. By using the F1-score, all model’s overall performances are evaluated to devise an informed conclusion that the proposed MTADC model is comparatively more efficient in real-world scenarios for apple leaf disease classification than other models (Table 6 ). The MTADC model consistently outperforms or matches competing models across all disease classes in terms of F1-score. Its performance is particularly notable for Powdery Mildew and Scab, where it demonstrates a clear edge over CNN, ResNet, and VGG-16. CNN shows competitive results for Mosaic and Scab but struggles for Alternaria and Powdery Mildew. ResNet and VGG-16 consistently exhibit lower F1 scores, making them less reliable for apple disease classification tasks.
Precision is a crucial metric in machine learning that measures the accuracy of positive predictions made by a model. It quantifies the proportion of true positive predictions among all instances predicted as positive by the model. A high precision score indicates that the model makes fewer false positive predictions, which is important in scenarios where the cost of false positives is high, such as in our case where false prediction can lead to incorrect treatment. Precision is particularly valuable when dealing with imbalanced datasets or when the focus is on the performance of positive predictions. It complements other metrics by providing insights into the model’s ability to avoid false positives and make accurate positive predictions. By evaluating precision, all model’s reliability for positive predictions is evaluated and the conclusion is devised that the proposed MTADC model is most suitable for the cause as compared to other models (Table 7 ). The MTADC model demonstrates outstanding precision for Powdery Mildew and Scab, while maintaining competitive performance for Alternaria and Mosaic. CNN occasionally surpasses MTADC in Alternaria and Mosaic precision, but its performance drops considerably for Powdery Mildew. ResNetandVGG-16generallylagbehind across all disease categories, suggesting their limited suitability for this task.
Accuracy curves display the progression of model accuracy over training epochs, showcasing how well the model is learning to make correct predictions. On the other hand, loss curves illustrate how the loss function changes during training, indicating how effectively the model minimizes the errors. Comparing these curves across different models it was found that the VGG16 and ResNet model initially performed well but over time there is quite a downfall and they saturated to lower accuracy, however in case of CNN it saturated early within 10 epochs whereas our multilayered transformer model took 30 epochs to saturate however the proposed MTADC model produced the most optimal result among all models tested. During the training of VGG16 and ResNet models, it was found that the models were training well for a few epochs and then there was a large downfall in accuracy and an increase in the loss. Around 10 epochs the models appeared to be converged however slight further training showed that the model was not converging and after prolonged training, we found that these models converged around 40 epochs. However, the CNN model and the proposed MTADC do not show such a pattern, and they converge without such downfalls. There are multiple noises in the background, and thus, while training, there is a possibility that the model may learn some noise features, resulting in reduced accuracy of the model. The multilayer transformer model is countering this by using multi head attention, and thus, there are no such downfalls in the proposed MTADC model. Moreover, the CNN model does not have multiple complex layers as in VGG16 and ResNet, so in CNN also, no such downfalls are observed.
The robustness of the proposed MTADC framework was evaluated using a hybrid dataset containing both field-acquired and public images captured under diverse environmental conditions. Field images were collected under natural orchard settings, exhibiting variations in illumination, background clutter, occlusions by branches and fruits, and partial leaf damage. Furthermore, data augmentation operations such as rotation and shearing were applied to simulate additional geometric distortions and occlusions. The proposed model demonstrated stable performance across all disease classes, indicating strong robustness against real-world imaging variability. These results suggest that MTADC can generalize effectively under practical horticultural monitoring scenarios.
Overall, the proposed MTADC model outperforms other models (as shown in Fig. 9 ) across all the classes. The results clearly establish the proposed MTADC model as the most robust and effective solution for apple leaf disease classification. Its high precision and F1-scores across all four disease classes, particularly for Powdery Mildew and Scab, highlight its superior ability to accurately classify diseases while minimizing false positives and negatives. CNN shows some strengths, particularly in Mosaic and Scab, but its performance drops noticeably for other diseases. ResNet and VGG-16 lag significantly behind, making them less suitable for this task. The MTADC model’s consistent performance and balanced results across precision and F1-score make it the most reliable choice for real-world disease detection in apple crops. The difference in performance is quite significant across all of the classes. Performance of other models were acceptable in some of the specific classes, but poor in other, which is not the case with the proposed MTADC model.

Although the proposed MTADC framework demonstrates strong performance, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the model was trained on four apple disease categories, and its ability to generalize to unseen disease classes remains to be explored. Second, the field-acquired dataset was collected from a specific geographic region, and variations in apple cultivars, orchard management practices, and environmental conditions across different regions may affect model performance.
Furthermore, extreme illumination conditions, severe occlusions, and sensor-specific artifacts were not systematically evaluated in this study. Future research will focus on large-scale multi-region data collection, domain adaptation, and continual learning strategies to improve generalization across diverse agro-climatic zones and apple varieties. Incorporating open-set recognition and few-shot learning frameworks could further enable detection of previously unseen disease patterns, enhancing the practical applicability of the proposed system.
The model is designed for real-world practical application i.e. to assist the farmers specifically smartphones with constrained compute resources and unreliable internet access. With the advancement and wide avail ability of the mobile phones, they are accessible to each and every hand. They serve as the ideal platform to deliver this solution to those in the agricultural sector. To facilitate this, a mobile application has been developed that act as an intermediate for accessing the model. The app features multiple interfaces (as depicted in Fig. 10 ) that allows users to upload images. Once an image is uploaded, it is transmitted to a server where the proposed model is hosted for processing.

In this work, a Multilayer Transformer-based Apple Disease Classification (MTADC) model is presented for robust and accurate classification of different apple leaf diseases in their early stages. The proposed MTADC model has demonstrated exceptional performance and surpassed established models like CNN, ResNet, and VGG16. Through rigorous training and hyperparameter tuning, we achieved an impressive accuracy of 96.32% on the testing set, with class-wise accuracies ranging from 92.84% to 99.58%. The stability of MTADC model’s training process, converging around 30 epochs, reflects its efficient learning capabilities. Our transformer architecture, with its Patch Encoder, transformer blocks, and dense layers, not only delivers superior accuracy but also offers scalability for potential enhancements. This model’s success opens doors for practical applications, particularly in fields like agricultural disease detection (evidenced by high accuracy across classes like Alternaria, Mosaic, Mildew, and Scab), where accurate image classification is paramount. The Multi Layer architecture of the presented model has not been explored to its maximum limit. The proposed model is presented only for the apple plants. However, to advance its reach in agriculture field, the model is required to be trained and tested across various variety of crops. As a future work, to fully utilize the proposed model’s capabilities, it needs to be analyzed on a wider domain of crops. The deployment-oriented design of MTADC enables practical adoption in real-field conditions through smartphone-assisted disease diagnosis.
