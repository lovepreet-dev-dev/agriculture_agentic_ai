TITLE: Genome assembly and comparative analysis ofAlternaria linariaereveal novel genes associated with host colonization and virulence
URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12232873/

Early blight (EB) is one of the most economically devastating diseases affecting tomato production, leading to significant yield losses. Traditionally, EB has been associated with the fungus Alternaria solani . However, recent evidence indicates that A. linariae (previously known as A. tomatophila ) is the main causative agent. This revised understanding improves diagnostic accuracy and underscores A. linariae as a valuable resource for studying host-pathogen interactions, host adaptation, and evolutionary mechanisms within the Alternaria genus due to its distinct pathogenic profile and host specificity.
The A. linariae strain 25, isolated from EB-infected tomatoes in Swain County, North Carolina, United States, was confirmed through analyses of its asexual morphs, cultural characteristics, and PCR-based identification. Nonetheless, the genomic structure and genetic factors influencing host specificity in A. linariae on tomatoes remain poorly understood. To address this knowledge gap, we sequenced the complete genome of A. linariae . This study will help elucidate its structure, compare its genomic features with those of other Alternaria spp., and identify the genetic basis for its host specificity. These insights are crucial for understanding its pathogenicity and host adaptation and for developing effective disease management strategies.
The genome of A. linariae strain 25 has been fully assembled and spans 33.1 Mb. It comprises 18 rRNA genes and 117 tRNA genes, and approximately 272,542 base pairs (0.8%) of repetitive elements. The genome features 11,768 predicted gene models, with 221 genes identified as potential candidate effector proteins that may play a role in host colonization and specificity. Additionally, the genome encodes 573 carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) and 37 secondary metabolite gene clusters, which are likely involved in pathogenicity and interactions with the host.
A comparative genomic analysis of effector genes across different Alternaria spp. revealed that the A. linariae strain 25 possesses unique effectors, which may enhance its ability to colonize and adapt precisely to tomato plants. These findings lay the groundwork for further investigation into the molecular mechanisms that drive host specificity and pathogenicity in A. linariae and provide a foundation for developing targeted strategies to control early blight in tomato crops.

Tomato ( Solanum lycopersicum L.) is the second most cultivated vegetable crop in the world, after the potato. However, optimized production is constrained by various fungal diseases, particularly early blight (EB) caused by Alternaria spp. (phylum Ascomycota, family Pleosporaceae) [ 1 , 2 ]. EB affects both potato and tomato plants, with a significant economic impact on tomato cultivation globally [ 1 ]. Common symptoms of EB include dark brown to black lesions, either small and irregular or large and regular, characterized by concentric necrotic rings on older leaves near the plant base and on stems, particularly at the soil line. On fruits, lesions show concentric rings, resulting in a “bull’s-eye” appearance. Severe infections can cause defoliation and affect stems and fruits [ 3 , 4 ]. If uncontrolled under favorable weather conditions, yield losses may reach up to 40% [ 5 ]. Furthermore, climate change is predicted to increase the prevalence of diseases caused by pathogens, including Alternaria spp [ 6 ]. Consequently, understanding the genetics and biology of Alternaria spp. is crucial for developing effective control strategies.
The genus Alternaria comprises over 360 species, categorized into 29 Sect. [ 7 ]. These fungi are known to be significant mycotoxigenic agents, producing various mycotoxins that pose risks to the safety of both fresh and processed tomato products [ 8 ]. Some mycotoxins also play a crucial role in the virulence of the pathogen to cause plant diseases [ 9 ]. The primary pathogens responsible for EB in tomatoes include A. linariae (Neerg.) E.G. Simmons (syn. A. tomatophila E. G. Simmons 2000 ( https://www.speciesfungorum.org/Names/SynSpecies.asp?RecordID=505056 accessed on 1 November 2024). Other species, such as A. cretica and A. subcylindrical [ 10 ], and small-spored species like A. alternata [ 11 ], have been isolated from affected tissues at low frequencies. Additionally, Alternaria crassa has been reported on tomatoes and other hosts [ 12 ].
Much of the earlier literature associated A. solani with EB of tomato. The delimitation between A. linariae and A. solani has proven challenging, as all large-spored Alternaria spp. were previously classified under A. solani Sorauer. Phylogenetic analysis using concatenated multigene sequences, which include the internal transcribed spacers 1 and 2 along with the 5.8 S gene (ITS), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase ( GAPDH ), the DNA-dependent RNA polymerase II gene ( RPB2 ), transcription elongation factor 1α ( TEF1 ), and the major Alternaria allergen gene ( Alt a 1 ), indicated that A. linariae , A. solani , and A. grandis cluster as a single clade [ 7 , 13 ]. However, single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the RPB2 have been identified as potential molecular markers to differentiate A. linariae from A. solani [ 14 ]. Previous genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS) analysis indicated that while A. linariae and A. solani are genetically closely related, their genomes have some notable differences [ 14 ]. These advancements have improved species identification, which was previously complicated by their morphological similarities.
Despite the significance of A. linariae as a devastating pathogen infecting tomato, the current morphological characteristics and molecular markers are insufficient for accurately distinguishing between species. Furthermore, there is a notable absence of comprehensive genomic resources for this pathogen, which is critically needed to support biosystematics and species diagnostics. While various studies have been conducted on the growth characteristics and genetic, pathogenic, and genomic variations of A. solani and A. alternata [ 15 , 16 ] similar research on A. linariae is limited, making it a promising candidate for investigating host adaptation and evolutionary processes within the Alternaria genus.
Currently, the only publicly available genome of A. tomatophila BMP2032, phylogenetically closely related to A. linariae is found in the JGI MycoCosm repository [ 17 ]. However, this genome is highly fragmented, consisting of 2,639 contigs, which offers only a partial view of the pathogen’s genomic architecture. Although the NCBI database contains numerous genome assemblies for small-spored Alternaria spp., only two were derived from tomato plants, and none represent large-spored Alternaria spp. The scarcity of genomic resources significantly hinders research into the genomic and molecular factors underlying pathogen biology, such as fungicide resistance and pathogen effector proteins, carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes), and secondary metabolite clusters. For example, A. solani and A. linariae are well adapted to solanaceous crops, such as potatoes and tomatoes. However, A. solani is aggressive towards both potato and tomato plants, whereas A. linariae is more aggressive specifically towards tomatoes [ 18 ] highlighting the need for a more thorough genomic study to understand its genetic makeup and mechanisms of host specificity. The pathogenicity of many Alternaria spp. has primarily been linked to the production of host-specific toxins (HSTs) [ 19 ]. These toxins induce disease in a relatively narrow range of host species and are often essential for pathogenicity. For example, AK-toxin produced by A. alternata apple pathotype induces necrosis in apples [ 20 ]. At least 12 pathotypes of A. alternata have been identified as producing HSTs, which can cause disease in various species, including tomato [ 19 ]. Given the significance of A. linariae and the lack of information on its genetic factors related to pathogenicity, our study aimed to: (1) sequence and annotate the genome of A. linariae strain 25, isolated from tomato; and (2) compare this genome with previously sequenced Alternaria spp. to identify genes linked to host specificity, including effector proteins, CAZymes, and secondary metabolites. This research provides valuable genomic resources that can aid future studies on reducing the impact of Alternaria diseases in agriculture. Our findings may help identify novel fungicide targets, support the development of resistant crop varieties, and enhance our understanding of fungal evolution and pathogenicity. Importantly, we present the first near-complete chromosomal assembly of the A. linariae genome, offering a comprehensive foundation for further studies.
In 2015, leaves from the tomato variety Plum Regal exhibiting symptoms of EB were collected in Swain County (Latitude: 35° 29’ 24” N; Longitude: −83° 29’24” W; and Elevation: 893 m), western North Carolina, USA. The fungus responsible for the disease was isolated and identified according to previously established methods [ 11 ]. First, the leaves were thoroughly rinsed with sterile water and air-dried. Next, they were disinfected using a 0.3% sodium hypochlorite solution for one minute, followed by surface sterilization with 75% alcohol for an additional minute. The leaves were then rinsed three times with sterile water before being air-dried. Once cleaned, the leaves were placed on potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium and incubated at 26 °C. After seven days, the samples were examined under a microscope at 40× magnification to identify asexual conidia. The conidia were carefully isolated using a sterile needle and subjected to three rounds of purification to obtain pure cultures derived from single spores. Finally, the strains were identified based on morphological characteristics and through amplifying the Alt a1 and calmodulin-encoding gene regions [ 11 ]. Strain 25 of A. linariae is representative of this species based on previous work [ 11 ].
Alternaria linariae strain 25 was cultured on PDA medium at 25 °C for 5 days and then at 20 °C for an additional 7 days. Genomic DNA was extracted from the harvested mycelia using a DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Genome sequencing of strain 25 was performed using the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (Novogene Corporation, Sacramento, CA, USA) to generate 150 bp paired-end reads.
The obtained reads were filtered using FastQC v0.11.9 [ 21 ] and Trimmomatic v0.38 [ 22 ] with default parameters. The genomes were then trimmed and assembled using SPAdes v4.0.0 [ 23 ] using the ‘–only-assembler’ flag and -k 21,33,55,77 for k-mer sizes. Contigs shorter than 500 bp were removed using SeqKit v0.10.1 [ 24 ]. Potential mitochondrial or bacterial regions were identified via BLASTn (v2.10) searches and discarded. The filtered reads underwent further processing and scaffolding using the Ragtag pipeline, with A. solani NL03003 [ 25 ] as the reference genome. The Burrow-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) -v0.7.18; [ 26 ]) and SAMtools [ 27 ] were employed to sort and index the BAM files alongside the reference genome. Pilon v1.23 [ 28 ] was used for polishing (error correction) to enhance the assembly further. The quality and integrity of the assembly, along with genome statistics, were evaluated using QUAST v5.0.2 [ 29 ]. To assess genome completeness, we utilized the Benchmarking Universal Single-copy Orthologs (BUSCO v5.5) tool with the fungal_odb10 and ascomycota_odb10 lineage datasets [ 30 ].
Repeated and low-complexity DNA sequences were identified using the conserved model sequences of putative scattered repeats obtained from RepeatModeler v1.0.4 ( http://repeatmasker.org/RepeatModeler/ ). The remaining sequences were compiled into a library for searching new scattered repeat sequences using RepeatMasker v4.0.5 ( http://www.repeatmasker.org/RepeatMasker/ ). Predictions for tRNA were made with tRNAscan-SE v1.3.1 ( http://lowelab.ucsc.edu/tRNAscan-SE/ ), while predictions for rRNA were conducted using RNAmmer v1.2 ( https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/RNAmmer-1.2/ [ 31 ]). Additionally, tandem repeats were identified using Tandem Repeat Finder [ 32 ].
A comparative genomic analysis was conducted using the genomes of 17 Alternaria spp., and genomic data were sourced from the Joint Genome Institute (JGI) MycoCosm and NCBI GenBank (Supplementary Table S 1 ). OrthoVenn3 was used to analyze protein sequences and identify orthologous protein groups from the 21 Alternaria spp [ 33 ]. Orthologous proteins were clustered, generating a species tree for the Alternaria sample. The analysis focused on the amino acid sequences of 5,740 single-copy core genes. The protein sequences were concatenated and aligned for tree construction using MAFFT version 7.215 [ 34 ]. Conserved block regions in each gene alignment were selected with Gblocks v0.91b [ 35 ] following the software’s default parameters. These blocks were then combined into a long sequence for each species. A maximum likelihood tree was constructed in MEGA v.11 using the GTR + I + G model [ 36 ] A multigene phylogenetic tree was built using locus-specific sequences for the following genes: ITS, ATPase, GAPDH , RPB2 , and TEF1 . The resulting phylogeny was applied for strain identification. Finally, the trees were visualized using iTOL v6 [ 37 ]. Additionally, we conducted an Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) analysis using the pyANI and MuMMER [ 38 ], with the results illustrated as a heatmap. Gene collinearity, which shows the preservation of ancestral genome structure, was performed by comparing the whole genome sequence of A. linariae strain 25 with the complete genomes of A. solani NL03003) and A. alternata Y784-B03 [ 39 ]. This comparison aimed to assess genetic identity, overlap, and variation through pan-genomic analysis, utilizing minimap2 (v2.24 [ 40 ]),, D-GENIES (v1.4, dot-plot software [ 41 ]),, and the SYNY pipeline [ 42 ].
For predicting putative genes, we used three software tools: AUGUSTUS version 3.4.0 [ 43 ], GlimmerHMM version 3.0.1 [ 44 ], and GeneMark-ES version 4.48 [ 45 ] were used for de novo genome modeling. GeneMark-ES was run in self-training mode, which allowed it to generate species-specific parameters directly from the Alternaria linariae genome sequence. AUGUSTUS was applied using a precomputed model derived from the closely related species Alternaria solani , GlimmerHMM utilized a training set from a closely related fungal taxon within the class Dothideomycetes, selected based on phylogenetic proximity. Evidence Modeler created a consensus gene set by integrating the output files from these three tools [ 46 ]. All predicted protein-coding genes of A. linariae and other Alternaria spp. retrieved from NCBI GenBank were functionally annotated using the Non-supervised Orthologous Groups database (eggNOG-mapper version 2.1.6) [ 47 ] and Gene Ontology (GO) [ 48 ]. This approach categorizes genes into functional groups based on orthology and offers insights into potential gene functions by mapping them to known gene families and biological pathways. Additionally, metabolic pathway analysis was conducted using BlastKOALA, referencing the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway database [ 49 ].
To identify gene clusters responsible for producing secondary metabolites, the predicted genes were analyzed using the Antibiotics and Secondary Metabolite Analysis (antiSMASH) tool ( https://fungismash.secondarymetabolites.org/#!/start ), focusing on core biosynthetic genes for further investigation. For the annotation of carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes), the dbCAN3 server [ 50 ] has been utilized, which uses a combination of three tools: HMMER (dbCAN2, E-Value < 1e-15), DIAMOND (E-Value < 1e-102), and Hotpep (peptide hits > 6 and frequencies > 2.6), along with the CGC-Finder was used to identify CAZyme gene clusters (CGCs). A gene was considered a CAZyme if at least two of the three tools yielded a positive annotation. Additionally, SignalP 6.0 [ 51 ] was used to identify the secretome, which was then used to identify putative effectors using EffectorP v.3.0 [ 52 ]. The predicted effectors were further refined using DeepTMHMM V1.0.24 [ 53 ] to remove proteins harboring transmembrane helices. Proteins with ≥ 1 predicted TMH were excluded. Genes associated with host-pathogen interactions were predicted via the PHI v4.8 database ( http://www.phi-base.org/ ), which comprises experimentally verified pathogenicity, virulence, and effector genes from bacterial and fungal pathogens that infect hosts such as plants, animals, fungi, and insects. BLASTP was employed with a cut-off E value of ≤ 1e − 06 in the pathogen-host interaction (PHI) database to identify probable pathogenicity-related genes [ 54 ]. The R package ggplot2 was used to visualize the distribution of CAZymes and effectors. In contrast, the heatmap implemented in R was used to visualize the SM gene clusters in the genome of A. linariae strain 25.
The assembled genome size of A. linariae strain 25 was 33,101,063 base pairs across 12 contigs, demonstrating high contiguity, with a mean N50 of 2.99 megabase pairs and a GC content of 51.69%. The genome coverage is 134×. When the assembled genome was aligned with databases for fungi and Ascomycota in BUSCO, a match rate of 98.9% was achieved (Table 1 ), indicating a high level of completeness. Additionally, repetitive elements accounted for 272,542 bp (0.82%) of the genome (Supplementary Table S 2 ).

Long terminal repeats (LTRs) were identified for the dispersed repetitive sequences, averaging 50 bp in length. The genome contained 36 DNA transposons (2,822 bp; 0.01%), 246 long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs; 18,332 bp; 0.06%), and 17 short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs; 1,177 bp). Interspersed repeats totaled 2,232 bp (0.04%). Additionally, 5,185 minisatellite DNAs (220,223 bp; 0.6%) and 485 microsatellite DNAs (24,419 bp; 0.07%) were identified (Supplementary Table S 2 ).
In total, 11,114 protein-coding genes, 18 rRNA genes, and 117 tRNA genes were predicted for the genome of A. linariae strain 25 (Table 1 ). Of these genes, 9,338 were categorized into Clusters of Orthologous Group/Eukaryotic Orthologous Group categories and 7,772 genes were assigned Pfam domains using eggNOGmapper version 4.5.1 (Table 1 ). The final genome assembly of A. linariae strain 25 comprises 10 chromosomes, each with telomeric repeats (TTAGGG) at one or both ends. The two unassigned contigs, which are part of the entire genome, were included in the analysis but lacked sufficient information for gene annotation (Table 2 ). The genomic data were deposited in the NCBI GenBank database under BioProject accession PRJNA1193289. The raw sequence data were submitted to NCBI SRA (accession number SRP550032; https://identifiers.org/ncbi/insdc.sra:SRP ), and the assembled genome is accessible at number JBJMB000000000.

The phylogenetic tree analysis of the whole genome revealed that A. linariae strain 25 is closely related to A. solani NL03003 and the A. tomatophila strain BMP2032, with over 98% bootstrap support (Fig. 1 ). The resulting phylogeny indicated that large-spored Alternaria spp. form a distinct, well-supported cluster, whereas small-spored Alternaria spp. are distributed across multiple, separate lineages. Similarly, phylogenetic analysis based on a combined dataset from five gene regions showed that A. linariae strain 25 clustered more closely with A. solani and A. tomatophila , which showed more than 98% bootstrap support (Supplementary Figure S 1 ). The tree topology was consistent with whole-genome analyses, further supporting the relationship among these species.

Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) analysis indicated a high genome sequence similarity between the A. linariae strain 25 and A. tomatophila BMP2032 (99%) and A. solani NL03003 (98%), suggesting they are closely related and may belong to the same species complex. In contrast, A. linariae strain 25 and A. brassicicola Abra43 have low ANI values (86%), indicating these species belong to distinct clades within the Alternaria genus (Fig. 2 ).

Furthermore, genome assemblies (Fig. 3 A) indicate discontinuous synteny between A. linariae and A. solani , with 60% of the genome in syntenic blocks highlighting significant structural rearrangements or repositioning between the two genomes. The alignment with A. alternata Y784-BC03 shows minimal overlap and sparse synteny, suggesting greater genomic variation and divergence (Fig. 3 B). These results are corroborated by dot plots generated using D-Genies, which reveal a high degree of synteny and conservation between A. solani NL03003 and A. linariae strain 25 (Fig. 4 A), suggesting close relationships. The alignment between A. linariae strain 25 and A. alternata Y784-BC03 is fragmented, indicating significant genomic rearrangements or divergence (Fig. 4 B). Chromosome 1 of A. linariae strain 25 aligns with multiple regions on the chromosomes of A. alternata . This suggests possible chromosomal fusions, fissions, or translocations in the evolutionary history of these species.

Further comparison of putative orthologous protein clusters between A. linariae strain 25 and other Alternaria spp. revealed that all species shared a core set of 6,364 clusters (Fig. 5 A), representing conserved orthologous genes across the genus. However, a smaller subset of clusters was shared among specific species; for instance, 162 clusters were uniquely shared among A. linariae , A. solani , and A. tomatophila , while 179 clusters were shared only between A. linariae and A. tomatophila . These species-specific clusters may reflect functional adaptations or evolutionary divergence among these closely related species.In addition, A. linariae strain 25 had 20 singletons (Fig. 5 C), of which 15 lack Gene Ontology (GO) annotation, suggesting that they may represent uncharacterized proteins. The remaining five clusters are associated with post-embryonic development (GO: 0009791), oxidoreductase activity (GO: 0016705, GO: 0016491), cellulose catabolic processes (GO: 0030245), and antibiotic biosynthesis (GO: 0017000).

The genome of A. linariae strain 25 features 11,768 predicted gene models. Functional annotation revealed 221 genes as putative candidate effector proteins potentially involved in host colonization and specificity. SignalP v6.0 analysis of the proteomes of A. linariae strain 25 revealed that 1003 predicted proteins contain a secretion signal peptide. The secretome, CAZymes, effectors, and secondary metabolite gene clusters of A. linariae strain 25 were determined and compared to those of other Alternaria species The CAZyme family genes predicted using the dbCAN2 meta server were divided into six main groups: glycoside hydrolases (GH), glycosyltransferases (GT), polysaccharide lyases (PL), carbohydrate esterases (CE), auxiliary activities (AA), and carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs). A total of 573 sequences encoding CAZymes were found within the genome of A. linariae strain 25 . The number of CAZyme genes in A. linariae strain 25 was in the same range as those predicted in A. dauci A2016, A. arborescens FERAA675, and A. alternata SRC1lrK2f (Fig. 6 A). Polysaccharide lyases and carbohydrate-binding modules were similar among the seven Alternaria spp. A. linariae has expanded CBM36, CBM37, CBM38, and CBM39 (Fig. 6 B). However, it lacks PL42, GH55, CAZymes, and AA6 and AA8 with auxiliary activities that are present in other Alternaria spp. used in this study (Fig. 6 B). The AA9 family is enlarged in all Alternaria spp. examined containing > 28 copies of the gene (Supplementary Table S3).

Effectors are proteins or small molecules secreted by pathogens that manipulate host cell processes, facilitating infection and suppressing host immunity. In this study, we identified 221 secreted effector candidates in A. linariae strain 25 (Fig. 7 ), including pectate and other polysaccharide lyases, glycoside hydrolases, a pathogenesis-related protein, a hypersensitive-inducing protein, peptidases, carbohydrate esterase, and several hypothetical proteins (Supplementary Table 3). The number of predicted effectors in the other Alternaria spp. ranged from 243 in A. solani to 248 in A. alternata , respectively. To identify potential species-specific pathogenicity and virulence factors, we analyzed unique clusters of A. linariae strain 25 and A. tomatophila BMP2032. Among the 273 genes identified from 179 clusters (Fig. 5 A), 17 genes were predicted to have signal peptides. Of these, seven were classified as effectors: two were apoplastic, two cytoplasmic, and three were hybrids of apoplastic and cytoplasmic. We also identified species-specific putative effector proteins, including the AA9 family (previously known as GH61), which consists of copper-dependent lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases (LPMOs) with the PFAM identifier PF03443, as well as a hypothetical protein. Further analysis using the PHI-base database revealed that eight of these effectors were associated with reduced virulence, five were linked to uninfected pathogenicity, and one each was related to reduced virulence, increased virulence (hypervirulence), and loss of pathogenicity (Supplementary Table S3 ).

EB, caused by the fungus A. linariae , is a significant disease that impacts tomato production globally. While using fungicides has had some success in managing this disease, the frequent application of these chemicals can lead to the development of new strains that are resistant to fungicides due to the strong selection pressure they create. For this reason, developing host resistance is the preferred strategy for sustainable disease management. However, the mechanisms underlying host plant resistance and their defense strategies against A. linariae in tomatoes are still not well understood. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted whole-genome sequencing and analyzed the genes that may be associated with pathogenicity and virulence. This research aims to enhance our understanding of the interaction between tomato plants and A. linariae , potentially leading to more effective management strategies. To the best of our knowledge, we have compiled draft genome resources of A. linariae , making these valuable tools available to the research community.
The assembled genome of A. linariae strain 25 is 33.1 Mb, with a GC content of 51.6% and an N 50 length of 2,993,250 kb. Notably, this assembled genome represents an improved version of A. tomatophila BMP2032. The genome size of A. linariae strain 25 is comparable to some sequenced genomes of A. solani NL300 (32.78 Mb) and A. alternata Y784-BC03 (32.30 Mb) [ 39 ], and A . porri Apn-Nashik (32.98 Mb) [ 58 ]. Isolate A. linariae strain 25 has a larger genome than A. tomatophila BMP2032 and contains 0.8% repeat elements, slightly below the range typically observed in other Alternaria spp. (1.2–2.7%; [ 17 ]). This repeat reduction may have resulted from trimming small contigs shorter than 500 bp, likely containing repetitive sequences.
In both phylogenetic analyses conducted in this study, A. linariae strain 25 clustered with A. tomatophila BMP2032 and A. solani NL03003 isolates 1). This relationship was corroborated with nucleotide-level genomic similarity of the whole genome sequence using ANI, as the three isolates, A. linariae strain 25, A. tomatophila BMP2032, and A. solani NL03003, exhibited high nucleotide similarity. This similarity can be explained by the fact that the ANI analysis-based overall genome includes all the contigs, significantly contributing to genetic diversity and plasticity, even among closely related strains [ 59 ]. A. solani and A. tomatophila (now known as A. linariae ) are sister species in phylogeny; both are necrotrophic pathogens that cause EB in potato ( Solanum tuberosum ) and tomato ( S. lycopersicum ) plants [ 4 , 13 ]. Although A. solani and A. linariae showed ANI values and phylogenetic relationships with strong support exceeding the 96% threshold indicative of species-level similarity [ 60 , 61 ] the two species can still be distinguished based on morphological traits, host specificity, and species-specific molecular markers [ 18 , 62 ]. Nevertheless, there remains a need for species re-evaluation and reclassification, supported by more comprehensive genomic, host range, and morphological comparisons involving multiple isolates [ 13 ]. Furthermore, while A. linariae has been synonymized with A. tomatophila and is generally considered the same species, the bootstrap support values in phylogenetic analyses were below 100%, likely due to the fragmented nature of the publicly available A. tomatophila genome (BMP2038; [ 17 ]). Comparative genome analysis showed differences in protein numbers among Alternaria spp. We predicted 11,114 protein-coding genes for A. linariae strain 25, aligning with other Alternaria isolates. A total of 1,003 secreted genes were identified, including 533 CAZymes, 221 effectors, and 37 from secondary metabolite clusters. Overall, these species formed 6,364 clusters, with A. linariae strain 25 sharing 11,142 clusters and 60 singletons. The shared clusters suggest that A. linariae may have similar pathogenicity determinants that could be targeted for a unified control strategy.
It is well-established that most necrotrophic pathogens secrete various carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) and toxins to degrade the plant cell wall [ 63 ]. Furthermore, these enzymes facilitate the active entry of pathogens into host cells [ 63 ]. Using carbon from plants indicates how effectively Alternaria can persist within host tissues before the manifestation of disease symptoms [ 64 ]. In the case of A. linariae strain 25, a distinctive absence of specific CAZyme families (PL42 and GH55) was observed that are present in closely related species. The CAZyme profile of A. linariae strain 25, especially the enrichment of glycoside hydrolases, which are typically involved in breaking down plant cell wall components like cellulose and hemicellulose, suggests a specialization in the degradation of plant cell walls, particularly in tomatoes, its primary host. Enriching CAZymes such as polysaccharide lyases and glycoside hydrolases enhances the pathogen’s capacity to penetrate host plant tissues and efficiently acquire nutrients by degrading cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin. This is consistent with the necrotrophic lifestyle of A. linariae , and we hypothesize that its colonization and growth depend on the breakdown of host tissues.
For A. linariae strain 25, 7 of the 30 AA9 proteins are predicted to be secreted effectors. Characterization of these CAZymes and their role in pathogenesis could be the subject of further studies. In addition, one of the AA9 gene families is unique to A. linariae . AA9 is involved in the degradation of cell-wall polysaccharides and is known to act on a range of polysaccharides, including starch, xyloglucan, cellodextrins, and glucomannan. LPMOs are believed to play a dual role: cleaving cell-wall polysaccharides directly and generating reactive oxygen species (ROS), which contribute to oxidative stress and necrosis in plant tissues [ 65 ].
The genera of Alternaria are known to be a major producer of host-specific secondary metabolite toxins [ 66 ]. Alternaria spp. are especially known for producing chemically diverse secondary metabolites, including host-specific toxins (HSTs) and non-HSTs [ 67 ]. Out of the 13 T1P KS clusters, we could identify two clusters that produce known secondary metabolites with a 100% match, 1,3,6, 8-tetrahydroxy naphthalene, and alternariol, with known roles in virulence. Gene clusters 1,3,6, 8-tetrahydroxy naphthalene were found in all strains. It serves as a precursor in the melanin biosynthesis pathway, which is critical for enhancing fungal survival under environmental stress (e.g., drought and oxidative stress), and it is likely to contribute to pathogenicity by strengthening appressoria for host penetration [ 67 , 68 ]. Alternariol has a cytotoxic and genotoxic effect and contributes to tomato pathogenicity, aiding tissue necrosis and nutrient acquisition [ 68 ].
Alternaria linariae strain 25 also had FR901512 , which matches 100% to an NRPS/T1PKS cluster. Despite being primarily recognized as an HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor, its role in fungal virulence and pathogenicity to plants is not well documented [ 67 , 68 ]. The presence of alternariol and alternapyrone in Alternaria spp. underscores their importance in the biology of this genus. Alternariol is a mycotoxin implicated in pathogenicity and toxicity, while alternapyrone may play a role in virulence or ecological competition [ 68 ]. Experimental studies are necessary to confirm the production of FR901512 in A. linariae strain 25 and elucidate its role in fungal biology or host interaction. Currently, there are no reports of FR901512 being produced by Alternaria spp.
Plant pathogens within the genus Alternaria have a dynamic ability to acquire new secondary metabolite potential to colonize new ecological niches [ 19 ]. The most plausible explanation for this dynamic acquisition of secondary metabolite potential is horizontal gene transfer within the Alternaria genus and possibly with other genera [ 66 ]. There is extensive evidence in the literature that many of the HSTs produced by Alternaria spp. are located on dispensable chromosomes, and the exchange of these chromosomes can broaden the host specificity [ 67 ]. However, the genome of A. linariae strain 25 was not predicted to contain any previously reported HST biosynthetic gene clusters from other Alternaria spp.
The genomic data from A. linariae strain 25 provides valuable insights into its host infection and pathogenesis mechanisms. Secreted effectors, enzymatic proteins, and virulence factors are the key players in any fungus’s lifecycle [ 69 ]. We annotated genes that were unique to A. linariae as previously described. The 278 genes were unique to A. linariae strain 25; only 17 contained signal peptides, and 6 were predicted effectors. When annotated with PHI, some genes were linked to known functions in pathogenicity or virulence (Supplementary Table S 5 ). Six were associated with reduced virulence, and two showed similarities to PopP2 ( Ralstonia solanacearum ) [ 70 ] and MoPtep1 ( Magnaporthe oryzae ) [ 71 ]Both of which were predicted as plant disease determinants. PopP2 targets host nuclear proteins, such as NB-LRR-immune receptors, to suppress plant immune responses [ 70 ]. At the same time, MoPtep1 encodes a pectin esterase that modifies pectin to aid in fungal penetration of the host plant cell wall [ 71 ]. An AA9 enzyme with auxiliary activities was unique to A. linariae. AA9 enzymes are a class of LPMOs catalyzing the oxidative cleavage of polysaccharides, such as cellulose, an essential step in plant biomass degradation [ 72 ]. LPMOs have been recognized as virulence factors in several necrotrophic fungi. For example, in Botrytis cinerea , AA9 family LPMOs have been shown to enhance virulence by degrading plant cell walls and releasing oligogalacturonides that modulate host defense signaling [ 65 ]. Similarly, in Sclerotinia sclerotiorum , LPMOs contribute to pathogenesis by facilitating the breakdown of host tissue and enabling deeper penetration into the plant apoplast [ 73 ]. We, therefore, hypothesize that the AA9 enzyme found in A. linariae may play a similar role in the adaptation of this species to tomatoes as a host, especially considering its unique presence compared to A. solani . To determine whether these putative species-specific effectors are crucial for defining the host range and tomato resistance, functional analyses are necessary. This includes examining gene expression during infection of host plants, creating targeted knockout mutants, and using effector proteins in assays to induce host-specific cell death. This research provides valuable insights into the factors that contribute to A. linariae’ s host specificity toward tomato plants. It provides a solid foundation for understanding the mechanisms behind A. linariae and its aggressive behavior towards tomatoes.
​This study presents the ​first draft genome of A. linariae strain 25, featuring unique carbohydrate-active enzyme families (CAZymes) CBM36- CBM39, and an expanded AA9 family, indicating its specialization in degrading tomato cell walls, consistent with its necrotrophic lifestyle. The genome also encodes significant secondary metabolites. ​The genome also encodes important secondary metabolites, such as alternariol and 1,3,6,8-tetrahydroxynaphthalene, which are crucial for virulence and stress tolerance. Phylogenetic analysis has confirmed a close relationship with A. solani , suggesting potential shared mechanisms of pathogenicity. These findings provide a foundation for understanding the pathogenicity of A. linariae and for developing targeted strategies to manage EB in tomatoes.
We can utilize genomic techniques such as RNA-sequencing, and CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing to gain insights into the molecular basis of host infection and enhance disease resistance in tomato plants. This approach is expected to lead to more effective disease management in the field. Furthermore, additional research is necessary, including comparative genomic analyses, functional gene studies, and metabolic profiling, to clarify the roles of potential effectors, enzymes, and secondary metabolites in the interactions between tomatoes and A. linariae .
