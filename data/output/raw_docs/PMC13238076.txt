TITLE: Dissecting heterosis and combining ability for yield and TYLCV resistance in tomato under field conditions
URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC13238076/

Tomato ( Solanum lycopersicum L.) is one of the most important horticultural crops worldwide, with yield and fruit quality strongly constrained by biotic stresses, particularly tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV). Hybrid breeding through the exploitation of heterosis is a highly effective approach for improving yield, fruit quality, and TYLCV resistance. To investigate the genetic basis of heterosis and trait inheritance, 17 determinate female and 7 semi-determinate male inbred lines were crossed in a North Carolina Design II (NCII) scheme, generating 119 F₁ hybrids evaluated alongside their parents and control cultivars. To ensure uniform and consistent TYLCV infection pressure, the highly susceptible cultivar ‘Matin’ was interplanted throughout the field as a spreader genotype.
Heterosis analysis revealed substantial positive better-parent heterosis (BPH) for yield-related traits, particularly single-plant yield and fruit number per plant, whereas negative heterosis was favorable for traits such as sunscald incidence, fruit acidity, and TYLCV severity. Genetic variance partitioning indicated that reproductive traits such as number of inflorescences on the main stem (NIMS), number of flowers per inflorescence (NFI), and number of fruits per inflorescence (NFPI) were predominantly governed by additive effects, suggesting that parental selection can efficiently improve these traits. In contrast, TYLCV resistance was strongly influenced by non-additive variance, highlighting the critical role of specific combining ability and hybrid interactions. Several hybrids combined high yield with complete resistance to TYLCV, underscoring the potential for simultaneous improvement of productivity and resilience.
This study not only clarifies the genetic basis of heterosis in tomato but also identifies elite parental lines and superior hybrids that can serve as valuable resources for breeding programs aimed at developing resilient, high-yielding cultivars under biotic and climatic stress conditions.

Tomato ( Solanum lycopersicum L.) stands among the most economically and nutritionally significant horticultural crops worldwide. Its widespread cultivation spanning over 5.4 million hectares globally reflects its central role in both subsistence and commercial agriculture. Iran, with approximately 77,000 hectares dedicated to tomato farming, an annual output surpassing 3.4 million tons, and an average yield of 45 tons per hectare (well above the global average of 38 tons per hectare), currently ranks as the 11th largest tomato producer globally [ 1 ].
One of the most effective breeding approaches for enhancing yield, fruit quality, and TYLCV resistance in tomato is the development of F₁ hybrids through the exploitation of heterosis, or hybrid vigor. Heterosis, a genetic phenomenon primarily expressed in the F₁ generation, tends to increase with greater genetic divergence between parental lines but typically diminishes in subsequent generations [ 2 ]. The effectiveness of this strategy is demonstrated by its widespread implementation in crops such as rapeseed, rice, and cotton, where hybrid cultivars account for over 90%, 70%, and 80% of total production, respectively [ 2 ]. Numerous studies have reported yield and quality enhancements of up to 60% in F₁ hybrids relative to their parental lines, with notable improvements in fruit uniformity, TYLCV resistance, and post-harvest shelf life [ 3 , 4 ]. In tomato, hybrid combinations involving phenotypically divergent parents particularly with respect to plant architecture and growth dynamics have exhibited significantly improved yields, largely due to favorable interactions during both vegetative and reproductive growth phases [ 5 ]. These advantages were primarily attributed to synergistic effects on key traits such as flower number, fruit weight, and soluble solids content. It has been shown that crosses between vigorous, well-balanced parental lines resulted in the highest levels of heterosis.
To identify elite parents and hybrid combinations with superior performance, a comprehensive genetic dissection involving general combining ability (GCA) and specific combining ability (SCA) is necessary. GCA reflects additive genetic variance and is instrumental in evaluating parental line performance, while SCA captures non-additive effects, including dominance and epistasis, and is closely linked to hybrid performance [ 6 , 7 ]. Although several studies, including Xiang et al. [ 8 ], have noted correlations between parental traits and GCA estimates, these metrics alone often fall short in accurately predicting heterosis levels. As such, robust statistical mating designs such as North Carolina Design II (NCII) or the Griffing method are indispensable tools for partitioning genetic variance and accurately estimating both GCA and SCA [ 9 ].
Tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV), a member of the Geminiviridae family and Begomovirus genus, is one of the most economically destructive pathogens affecting Solanum lycopersicum cultivation globally, especially in major tomato-producing countries such as China, Italy, Egypt, Iran, and the United States [ 1 , 10 ]. While TYLCV can be transmitted through seeds [ 11 – 13 ], its primary vector is the whitefly, Bemisia tabaci , whose population density significantly correlates with TYLCV outbreaks [ 14 ]. Infected plants exhibit symptoms such as severe stunting, chlorosis, leaf curling, flower drop, and reduced fruit quality [ 15 , 16 ]. Notably, B. tabaci thrives in high-temperature environments, further exacerbating TYLCV spread in warm climates [ 17 ]. Integrated management relies on host resistance, quarantine of infected material, and vector control [ 18 ].
Given the mounting challenges of climate change, including increased temperatures and intensified outbreaks of TYLCV, the development of resistant, high-yielding tomato hybrids has become an urgent breeding priority particularly in regions like Iran where agroclimatic conditions strongly favor virus proliferation [ 19 , 20 ]. Numerous studies have established a direct link between rising temperatures, expanding B. tabaci populations, and the geographic and epidemiological spread of TYLCV [ 17 ]. Climate projection models further predict the intensification of this issue under future scenarios, underscoring the importance of breeding strategies that emphasize both durable resistance and climate adaptability [ 21 ]. The market value of tomato hybrids with TYLCV resistance is not comparable to that of susceptible hybrids, and most farmers avoid cultivating susceptible varieties.
Building upon the theoretical framework proposed by Krieger et al. [ 5 ], who identified the SINGLE FLOWER TRUSS (SFT) gene as a key determinant of heterosis in tomato, parental lines in this study were deliberately selected based on contrasting determinate and semi-determinate growth habits and vegetative vigor to align with the dosage-dependent overdominance model underlying yield heterosis. Krieger et al. [ 5 ] demonstrated that heterozygosity at the SFT locus can increase fruit yield by up to 60% through a dosage-dependent balance between flowering and vegetative growth. This finding provided the first direct evidence of single-gene overdominance for yield in crops and underpins the objectives of the present study.
Although a considerable body of research has investigated TYLCV resistance genes, claimed resistant genotypes should be evaluated under natural field conditions with uniform virus pressure. All hybrids and lines were evaluated in a farm with uniform incidence of TYLCV. A very susceptible genotype (Matin) was cultivated in different parts of the farm to spread the TYLCV uniformly across the lines and hybrids which were cultivated for resistance evaluation. Finding the most resistant hybrids was the main purpose of this research. However, the next aim was to evaluate the heritability of important traits in tomato. This study thus aimed to conduct an in-depth genetic analysis of heterosis, variance components, heritability, and combining abilities for 18 agronomic and quality-related traits. In addition, genetic parameters of four key reproductive traits including the number of inflorescences per plant, the number of flowers per inflorescence, the number of fruits per inflorescence, and resistance to TYLCV were evaluated using the NCII crossing scheme. The overarching goal was to identify high-performing parental inbred lines and F₁ hybrid combinations that exhibit superior agronomic performance, resilience under climate stress, and stable pathogen resistance, thereby contributing to the development of elite commercial hybrids in the face of ongoing climatic and biological challenges.
In this study, a total of 24 selected parental inbred lines (F₆), including 17 female and 7 male lines, were used to analyze combining ability and heterosis in tomato ( Solanum lycopersicum L.). These lines were derived from six generations of selfing and selection, developed as part of a breeding program conducted at two experimental stations affiliated with the University of Tehran. Specifically, the inbred lines have been continuously developed, selected, and evaluated over the past ten years at the College of Abouraihan, University of Tehran, as part of a long-term tomato breeding program. In addition to differences in growth habit and plant architecture, the parental inbred lines were selected based on their contrasting phenotypic performance for key agronomic and stress-related traits. An initial population of 650 tomato inbred lines was developed using the single-seed descent (SSD) method and evaluated under field conditions across two contrasting environments. Pakdasht, characterized by high natural pressure of whiteflies and TYLCV, was used to assess virus response, while Karaj provided favorable conditions for evaluating yield and agronomic performance. During the first screening cycle, eight plants per line were evaluated for TYLCV severity under natural infection in Pakdasht, and the same lines were assessed for yield-related traits in Karaj. Lines exhibiting a TYLCV severity score greater than 3 on a 0–5 scale were eliminated regardless of yield performance. Through this dual-environment phenotypic screening, 50 lines were selected from the initial population. These lines were subsequently cultivated in greenhouse for hybrid development. At the greenhouse due to high infestation of whitefly, TYLCV symptoms were observed during plant growth. This was another chance to detect susceptible lines and led to further elimination of TYLCV-susceptible inbred lines. Ultimately, 24 parental inbred lines (17 female and 7 male) were retained, each having undergone at least two independent phenotypic screenings for TYLCV tolerance and agronomic suitability. These materials are not publicly registered in germplasm databases and therefore do not have assigned accession numbers. The classification of male and female parental lines was carried out based on contrasting phenotypic traits in vegetative growth and plant architecture, to maximize heterosis effects in line with the theoretical framework proposed by Krieger et al. [ 5 ]. However, the parental lines were selected based on phenotypic performance and breeding relevance rather than prior molecular characterization of flowering-related loci. Therefore, interpretations related to the SFT locus in this study are grounded in established theoretical models and observed phenotypic outcomes, rather than direct genotypic confirmation.
In November 2022, controlled crosses were performed following the North Carolina Design II (NCII), using 17 female lines (determinate growth type) and 7 male lines (semi-determinate or vegetative growth type) in the Golfam greenhouse complex (Pakdasht, Iran). Parental seeds were sown in seedling trays filled with a cocopeat–perlite substrate, and seedlings were transplanted to greenhouse beds after the emergence of the second true leaf. Greenhouse conditions were maintained at a temperature of 24–26 °C during the day and 18–20 °C at night, with relative humidity ranging from 45 to 55%. Plants were grown under natural daylight conditions. For hybridization, as female flowers mature earlier than male flowers, female buds were emasculated 2–3 days before anticipated anthesis. Flowers were selected when the petals were white. Emasculation was performed during the day time using sterilized forceps. Next day in the morning (around 9 o’clock) pollen from fully opened male flowers was collected into microtubes and then manually applied to the stigmas of the emasculated female flowers. Pollinated flowers were labeled, and, when necessary, excess flowers on the same inflorescence were removed to ensure accurate crossing. Approximately 10 flowers were pollinated per each planned cross to achieve a minimum of 5–6 successful fruit sets. Each line was grown with multiple plants (10 plants per inbred line) to maximize the chance of successful hybridization. The overall success rate of crosses was approximately 60%. Mature fruits were harvested at full physiological ripeness, and seeds were extracted via fermentation, followed by washing and drying. A total of 119 hybrids (7♂ × 17♀) were successfully obtained. The contrasting parental lines (determinate × semi-determinate) provided an opportunity to evaluate heterosis expression in relation to vegetative reproductive trade-offs, similar to the model proposed for SFT heterozygotes [ 5 ].
In 2023 summer growing season, a total of 1,304 individual plants, including 24 parental inbred lines, 119 F₁ hybrids, and four commercial control cultivars (Bedero, Brivio, 8320, and Matin), were evaluated at two research stations located in Karaj and Pakdasht. For each genotype, eight plants were grown per plot. According to the augmented design, only the four commercial control cultivars were replicated within each station. Each control cultivar was included once in each of the five blocks per station, resulting in a total of 20 observations (4 control cultivars × 5 blocks) per station. In contrast, the parental inbred lines and F₁ hybrids were evaluated without replication.
Seeds were sown in seedling trays containing a cocopeat–perlite mixture, and the seedlings were transplanted to the field at the 2–3 leaf stage. Row spacing was set at 1.5 m, with 40 cm between plants. The Karaj station, with favorable environmental conditions and fertile soil, was used to evaluate fruit yield and quality traits, whereas the Pakdasht station, characterized by higher temperatures and a natural population of whiteflies (vectors of TYLCV), was used to screen for resistance to TYLCV. To ensure uniform natural infection pressure, the highly susceptible cultivar “Matin” was planted throughout the entire field and among all plots, allowing for widespread and homogeneous virus spread (Supplementary Fig. S3). This natural field setup also provided the basis to test whether heterosis for yield and resistance could be partly explained by the same overdominant mechanisms identified for the SFT locus, particularly through enhanced reproductive performance under stress conditions [ 5 ].
Resistance to TYLCV was evaluated individually in all 1,304 single plants (eight plants per genotype were evaluated individually, and the scores were averaged). All 119 F₁ hybrids were included in combining ability and genetic variance analyses for TYLCV score, as complete datasets were available for these traits across all hybrids. TYLCV symptoms were recorded using a standard 0–5 scale (22): (0) No symptoms; (1) Leaf yellowing, size reduction, and mild downward curling; (2) Leaf thickening, pronounced downward curling, and internode shortening; (3) Severe curling and abnormal stem growth; (4) Severe wrinkling, stunting, and edge cupping; (5) Extreme stunting and total yield loss. Each genotype was scored at the peak of whitefly infestation, 60 days after transplanting (DAT), when plants had fruits at the onset of ripening stage and disease symptoms were fully expressed. The mean TYLCV score of the eight plants per genotype was calculated and treated as a continuous variable for subsequent combining ability, heterosis, and variance component analyses, which is a common practice in quantitative genetic studies of disease resistance.
Following field establishment, a two-stage evaluation strategy was implemented. All 119 F₁ hybrids were included in combining ability and genetic variance analyses for three reproductive traits, including the number of inflorescences on the main stem (NIMS), number of flowers per inflorescence (NFI), and number of fruits per inflorescence (NFPI), as complete datasets were available for these traits across all hybrids. These reproductive traits were emphasized because they represent key components of yield heterosis in tomato, as previously shown by Krieger et al. [ 5 ], who linked SFT heterozygosity to increased inflorescence and flower production. Subsequently, a subset of 51 F₁ hybrids was selected for heterosis analysis and detailed evaluation of 18 agronomic and fruit quality traits. This selection was based on preliminary field-based ranked assessments conducted by three experienced tomato breeding specialists. Selection criteria included plant vigor and health, canopy coverage related to sunscald tolerance, fruit firmness, fruit shape and marketability, and overall yield potential. Traits were ranked using an ordinal scoring system (1 = poor, 2 = intermediate, 3 = desirable), and only hybrids with high agronomic relevance and average ranking scores above the predefined threshold (2.5) were retained for further laboratory and field evaluations.
Agronomic and fruit quality traits were evaluated in Karaj under open-field conditions. Single-plant yield (SPY, kg/plant), fruit number per plant (FNPP), and single fruit weight (SFW, g) were determined based on cumulative data from three consecutive harvests, as fruits did not ripen simultaneously. The maturity dates of the genotypes varied, ranging from 107 to 136 days after transplanting (DAT). At each harvest, all marketable fruits (full physiological maturity) from all eight plants were collected and their total number and total weight were recorded for each genotype. SPY was calculated as the total fruit weight divided by the number of remaining healthy plants. FNPP was calculated by dividing the total number of fruits by the number of remaining healthy plants. SFW was then calculated as the ratio of SPY to FNPP. Trait evaluation and scoring procedures followed the guidelines of the International Union for the Protection of New Varieties of Plants (UPOV) for tomato [ 22 ]. Sunscald incidence was evaluated as the percentage of sunscald-affected fruits among 100 randomly harvested fruits per genotype, whereas persistent calyx in fruits (PCF) was recorded as the percentage of fruits retaining the calyx at harvest. For fruit quality and morphological traits, sampling was performed to minimize positional effects by collecting fruits from plants located in the central area of each plot. At the red-ripe stage (full physiological maturity), five representative fruits per genotype were harvested and transferred to the laboratory for detailed measurements. Fruit length (FL, mm), fruit width (FW, mm), and pericarp thickness (PTH, mm) were measured using digital calipers. The number of locules was determined as the mean of five fruits per genotype. Fruit firmness was evaluated using a manual sensory assessment. Five ripe fruits per genotype were assessed by multiple trained evaluators through gentle hand pressure and scored on an ordinal scale (1 = soft, 2 = intermediate, 3 = firm). Fruit juice pH was measured using a calibrated digital pH meter. Titratable acidity (TA) was determined by titrating 10 mL of freshly extracted juice, diluted to 100 mL with distilled water, using 0.1 N NaOH to an endpoint of pH 8.2. Titratable acidity was expressed as grams of citric acid equivalent per 100 mL of juice [ 23 ]. Total soluble solids content was measured as °Brix using a digital refractometer. Fruit shelf life (SHL) was evaluated using five healthy fruits per genotype stored at 25 °C in a well-ventilated room under ambient conditions and protected from direct sunlight. Fruit condition was monitored daily, and the number of days until visible deterioration or loss of marketable quality was recorded as the shelf-life index.
Data were analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) based on an augmented randomized complete block design. For traits evaluated across check cultivars, mean comparisons were conducted using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT) at the 5% probability level. To account for block effects and improve genotype comparisons, data were analyzed using ANOVA with control-based adjustments. Continuous quantitative traits were assessed using standard ANOVA procedures after verifying model assumptions (e.g., residual normality), while non-parametric traits, including count and ordinal data, were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test.

Analysis of variance conducted on the control cultivars revealed that block effects were not statistically significant for any of the evaluated traits, including TYLCV severity. Therefore, no block-based correction was required for the performance of parental lines or hybrids across blocks, indicating uniform natural infection pressure for TYLCV throughout the experimental field. The block effects for the other evaluated traits were non-significant as well. Disease severity scores among genotypes ranged from 0 to 5, with an overall mean of 1.088, confirming that the level of infection pressure was sufficient to reliably discriminate between resistant and susceptible responses. The susceptible control cultivar ‘Matin’ exhibited the highest mean disease severity score (2.764), confirming its effectiveness as a spreader genotype and ensuring homogeneous virus distribution under field conditions. In contrast, the remaining genotypes showed greater resistance and contributed less to overall TYLCV incidence (Fig. 1 ). The contrasting responses of tomato genotypes to TYLCV infection are illustrated in Fig. 2 , where the severe disease symptoms observed in the susceptible cultivar ‘Matin’ are compared with the symptom-free resistant hybrids.

The heterosis analysis revealed two distinct categories of traits: (i) those for which positive heterosis values are desirable (e.g., yield, fruit number, and fruit size–related traits), and (ii) those where negative heterosis indicates improvement (e.g., sunscald incidence, TYLCV resistance, and fruit acidity–related trait). Within the first category, the highest levels of positive heterosis were observed for SPY, with a mean MPH of 40.10% and more than 90% of hybrids displaying positive MPH. Similarly, FNPP and FW showed consistently high positive heterosis values, underscoring the strong potential of hybridization for yield enhancement. In the second category, where negative heterosis was favorable, notable patterns emerged. For sunscald incidence, mean heterosis values were − 25.05% (MPH) and − 50.06% (BPH). Strikingly, over two-thirds of the hybrids (68.6% for MPH and 76.5% for BPH) showed significant reductions in sunscald, highlighting the potential of hybrid combinations to improve fruit quality under light-stress conditions. TYLCV resistance, a key trait in this study, exhibited a wide heterosis range (− 100% to + 620% for MPH). Although the mean heterosis was modestly positive for MPH (8.71%) and negative for BPH (− 24.49%), more than half of the hybrids (52.95% for MPH and 56.87% for BPH) displayed negative heterosis, indicating enhanced resistance compared with both parents. Notably, 24 hybrids exhibited − 100% heterosis, representing a valuable outcome from a breeding perspective. For internal fruit quality attribute, negative heterosis was also advantageous. Fruit pH displayed high frequencies of negative heterosis: 54.9% of hybrids for MPH, and 80.4% for BPH. These results suggest that hybridization can effectively improve flavor quality and achieve a more balanced acidity profile in tomato fruits. Interestingly, although traits such as NFI and NFPI exhibited predominantly negative heterosis in our study, this pattern aligns with the theoretical framework proposed by Krieger et al. [ 5 ], who showed that yield heterosis in tomato is largely driven by an increase in NIMS rather than enhanced productivity within individual inflorescences. Consistent with this model, we observed strong positive heterosis for SPY and FNPP, indicating that reproductive unit proliferation is the main contributor to yield improvement. For example, hybrids from ♂73 × ♀50 and ♂73 × ♀151 produced four inflorescences versus two in their parents, while hybrids from ♂323 × ♀332 and ♂323 × ♀290 produced five versus three, clearly demonstrating heterotic effects. These findings support the dosage-dependent mechanism described by Krieger et al. [ 5 ], whereby heterozygosity fine-tunes the balance between vegetative and reproductive growth, ultimately enhancing floral productivity. The analysis of variance based on the North Carolina II (NCII) crossing design revealed differential and statistically significant contributions of male (m), female (f), and their interaction (m × f) effects to the variation observed in floral and virus resistance traits (Table 2 ). For NIMS and NFI, both male and female effects were significant ( p < 0.05), whereas the male × female interaction was non-significant, indicating that additive genetic effects predominantly control these traits. This suggests that selecting superior parents with strong GCA is more effective than relying on specific hybrid combinations. Interestingly, for NFPI, although both parental effects were significant, the absence of interaction again underscored the predominance of additive variance, with a stronger contribution from male parents. These findings are consistent with the model of Krieger et al. [ 5 ], which proposed that tomato yield heterosis is primarily driven by increased inflorescence number rather than per-inflorescence productivity. In contrast, TYLCV resistance exhibited highly significant male, female, and interaction effects ( p < 0.01), highlighting the involvement of both additive and non-additive gene actions. The significant interaction term reflects the importance of SCA and possible epistasis, suggesting that hybrid breeding is essential to achieve durable resistance. Therefore, both careful parental selection and targeted hybrid development are essential for improving TYLCV resistance.

The variance component analysis (Table 3 ) revealed contrasting genetic architectures across traits. For NIMS and NFI, additive variance slightly exceeded non-additive variance, with dominance degrees (1.26 and 1.19) indicating partial to full overdominance. Both h² and H² were high, suggesting that selection of elite parents with strong GCA, combined with hybrid breeding, can efficiently improve these traits. For NFPI, additive variance was much higher than non-additive variance, with D < 1 (0.58), consistent with incomplete dominance and strong additive control. This indicates that direct selection in early generations and recurrent selection in later cycles are effective strategies. Notably, for TYLCV resistance, the non-additive variance (4.11) was much greater than the additive component (0.94), with a high dominance degree (2.95), reflecting a pattern of strong overdominance. Despite a moderate h N ² of 0.43, the high H B ² value of 0.99 indicates that genetic factors account for most of the phenotypic variance (Fig. 6 ). These findings underscore the importance of non-additive gene action and epistasis, suggesting that hybrid breeding and heterosis exploitation are the most efficient approaches for improving resistance to TYLCV. These results align with the model of Krieger et al. [ 5 ], in which yield heterosis in tomato is largely explained by dosage-dependent effects on reproductive proliferation. Similarly, the predominance of additive variance for inflorescence traits supports the idea that heterozygosity at key loci enhances reproductive unit number, while the strong non-additive control of TYLCV resistance emphasizes the strategic value of exploiting SCA in hybrid development under biotic stress.

The identified parents, therefore, represent promising candidates for multi-trait hybrid breeding, combining high reproductive potential with durable virus resistance.

Specific combining ability (SCA) was analyzed to capture non-additive genetic effects and identify high-performing parental combinations for four key traits: NFI, NFPI, NIMS, and TYLCV severity (Fig. 9 ). Heatmaps of SCA values revealed substantial variation in parental interactions across traits, underscoring the critical role of non-additive effects in tomato hybrid performance. For TYLCV resistance, negative SCA values were favorable, and several hybrid combinations (451 × 176, 150 × 151, and 150 × 548) exhibited highly negative SCA, highlighting their potential as virus-resistant hybrids. For NFPI, which reflects successful fruit set, crosses such as 554 × 598, 150 × 240, and 323 × 151 showed superior SCA, suggesting enhanced reproductive efficiency. Similarly, for NFI, hybrids 323 × 151, 150 × 240, 554 × 164, and 554 × 420 displayed positive SCA, indicating strong flowering capacity. In the case of NIMS, combinations such as 323 × 176, 554 × 52, and 554 × 151 showed high positive SCA, reflecting enhanced inflorescence production. Overall, hybrids exhibiting favorable SCA across multiple traits represent multi-trait candidates for targeted breeding programs. Importantly, the strong SCA effects for TYLCV resistance reinforce the role of epistasis and overdominance, consistent with the theoretical framework proposed by Krieger et al. [ 5 ]. This suggests that heterozygosity at key loci not only enhances reproductive growth but also contributes to improved stress tolerance and TYLCV resistance by balancing resource allocation between yield and defense. To explore the genetic relationships among reproductive and resistance traits at the level of non-additive effects, Spearman’s correlation analysis was conducted using SCA values for NFI, NFPI, NIMS, and TYLCV severity. The resulting heatmap (Fig. 11 a) revealed a strong positive and significant correlation between NFI and NFPI ( r = 0.55), indicating genetic alignment between flowering capacity and fruit set efficiency in hybrids. Weaker, non-significant positive correlations were also detected between NIMS and other reproductive traits (e.g., NIMS vs. NFPI, r = 0.14), suggesting that inflorescence structure may modestly influence yield potential. In contrast, TYLCV resistance showed negative and non-significant correlations with reproductive traits (e.g., NFPI, r = − 0.12), pointing to genetic independence between resistance mechanisms and yield-related attributes. This independence is advantageous for breeding programs, as it allows simultaneous improvement of yield and resistance without genetic conflict, and is consistent with the model of Krieger et al. [ 5 ], where heterosis is driven mainly by reproductive proliferation rather than stress-related traits. In addition, correlations between mean GCA and SCA effects were assessed to examine their interdependence (Fig. 11 b). Across all traits, correlations were low and statistically non-significant, with the highest observed in NFPI ( r = 0.12) and the lowest in NFI ( r = − 0.04). These findings highlight that GCA does not reliably predict SCA, underscoring the necessity of jointly considering both components when selecting parents and designing crosses.

The TYLCV resistance network revealed that a subset of male and female parents consistently contributed to resistant hybrids, underscoring the importance of specific parental genotypes in conferring virus resistance through non-additive interactions. In contrast, for NIMS and NFI, certain parents were repeatedly associated with positive SCA effects, highlighting favorable additive × dominance interactions that promote inflorescence initiation and flowering. The NFPI network displayed a more balanced contribution from both parental groups, indicating complementary gene action in fruit set. These findings complement the ANOVA and variance component analyses, confirming that while additive effects shape baseline performance, non-additive interactions largely determine hybrid superiority. From a theoretical perspective, the recurrent appearance of certain high-contributing parents is consistent with the dosage-dependent mechanism proposed by Krieger et al. [ 5 ], where heterozygosity fine-tunes the balance between vegetative and reproductive growth. Overall, these network analyses provide a clear graphical overview of SCA patterns and highlight parental combinations most influential for trait improvement, thereby offering a practical decision-making tool for designing future hybrid breeding programs. The NCII-based analysis of variance in this study revealed that the patterns of genetic control varied across the evaluated traits. Flowering traits, including NIMS, NFI, and NFPI, were predominantly governed by additive genetic effects, as indicated by significant male and female parental effects and the absence of significant male × female interactions. In contrast, TYLCV resistance exhibited significant effects of both parents and their interaction, indicating the involvement of both additive and non-additive genetic components, including SCA. These findings suggest that while selection of superior parents is effective for improving flowering traits, hybrid development is essential for enhancing TYLCV resistance, which is consistent with previous reports [ 25 , 26 ].
Genetic variance estimates further highlighted these differences. TYLCV resistance displayed strong overdominance (D = 2.95) and very high broad-sense heritability (H² = 0.99), reflecting a decisive role for non-additive effects (σ²_SCA = 4.11). These results confirm the critical role of dominance and epistatic interactions in conferring resistance and support the application of hybrid breeding and parent selection based on SCA [ 27 – 29 ]. Reproductive traits NIMS and NFI showed a moderate degree of dominance with substantial additive contributions, confirming that hybrid breeding strategies should balance parental selection and heterosis exploitation [ 30 ]. In contrast, NFPI exhibited low dominance (D = 0.58) and the lowest broad-sense heritability (H²B = 0.67), indicating primarily additive control and higher environmental sensitivity, thus favoring direct selection in later generations [ 31 ]. This partitioning of genetic control aligns with the dosage-dependent model of Krieger et al. [ 5 ], which proposed that heterosis for yield arises largely from additive effects that increase the number of reproductive units, whereas resistance and stress tolerance rely more on non-additive interactions.
Both GCA and SCA were important for the studied traits (NFI, NFPI, NIMS, and TYLCV resistance), but their relative contributions differed, consistent with previous reports [ 2 , 28 ]. GCA were particularly prominent for total yield and vegetative traits, ensuring trait stability across generations [ 28 ], whereas SCA effects predominated in several traits, especially those associated with fruit set and early yield, underscoring the importance of exploiting heterosis [ 29 , 32 ]. These findings are in line with the present results and indicate that selecting parents solely based on high GCA without considering SCA does not guarantee superior hybrids. The predominance of non-additive genetic variance and high SCA for TYLCV resistance observed in this study indicates that resistance expression is strongly dependent on specific parental combinations. Although molecular markers were not used to identify specific resistance genes in the present study, the lack of association between yield components and TYLCV resistance suggests that the resistance observed in selected hybrids is independent of yield. These patterns are consistent with previous reports showing that major TYLCV resistance genes such as Ty-1 and Ty-3 confer virus resistance without yield penalties [ 33 , 34 ]. These findings support the effectiveness of hybrid breeding strategies for improving TYLCV resistance without compromising yield performance.
The integration of GCA and SCA results provided further insights. For example, strong positive heterosis for SPY and FNPP was observed in crosses where both parents had contrasting growth habits, consistent with the framework proposed by Krieger et al. [ 5 ]. This reflects the idea that heterozygosity fine-tunes the balance between vegetative and reproductive growth, leading to higher yield potential. In contrast, TYLCV resistance was maximized in crosses where non-additive effects predominated, confirming that defense-related traits benefit more from hybrid-specific genetic interactions than from additive parental contributions.
The Spearman correlation analysis between mean GCA and SCA values for reproductive traits and TYLCV resistance showed very weak and non-significant associations. This indicates that relying solely on GCA to predict hybrid performance is insufficient, a finding consistent with Liu et al. [ 2 ], who reported no significant GCA–SCA correlation across eight tomato traits, and with Su et al. [ 35 ], who emphasized the lack of continuity between these components. Moreover, line × tester analyses have indicated that high SCA combinations do not necessarily originate from high-GCA parents, but often from those with low to medium GCA [ 36 ]. Therefore, simultaneous evaluation of both GCA and SCA is essential for parent selection and precise breeding design.
This study also revealed a relatively strong positive and significant correlation ( r = 0.55) between NFI and NFPI, suggesting that increased reproductive capacity of the inflorescence can effectively enhance fruit set. This finding is consistent with Wijaya et al. [ 37 ], who reported in Jatropha curcas that inflorescence structure was a key determinant of fruit production. Moreover, no negative correlation was detected between TYLCV resistance and reproductive traits, indicating that simultaneous breeding for high yield and TYLCV resistance is feasible [ 33 , 34 ]. Identification of genotypes with high flowering and fruiting capacity, especially under heat and drought stress, may substantially improve yield potential [ 38 ].
Our findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that, in tomato, dominance for fruit size traits often favors small-fruit alleles. For instance, the small-fruit allele fw2.2 acts in a dominant or semi-dominant manner, significantly reducing fruit weight even in the presence of large-fruit alleles [ 39 , 40 ]. On the other hand, recent studies have demonstrated that this pattern can be more complex; for example, the large-fruit allele of fw6.3 exhibits overdominance, producing F1 fruits heavier than both parents [ 41 ], whereas the small-fruit allele of fw3.3 sometimes shows a reversed effect, even contributing to increased fruit weight [ 42 ]. Moreover, recent GWAS analyses have confirmed the existence of diverse dominance patterns among QTLs controlling fruit weight [ 43 ]. In line with this evidence, our data also showed positive mean heterosis for FNPP, while BPH for SFW was negative. This pattern likely reflects a source–sink trade-off, where an increase in fruit number per plant is generally accompanied by a reduction in individual fruit weight. Overall, these results indicate that both the genetic dominance of small-fruit alleles and physiological constraints in resource allocation play key roles in shaping the observed heterosis patterns.
Finally, several superior hybrids were identified that combined high yield potential with TYLCV resistance. Some of these exhibited complete resistance and strong positive heterosis for SPY, providing practical evidence that the dosage-dependent model of Krieger et al. [ 5 ] can be extended beyond controlled experiments to field-based breeding programs. Our results are consistent with recent reports highlighting the predominant role of non-additive effects and the feasibility of simultaneously enhancing yield and resistance [ 2 , 32 , 33 ]. Together, these findings highlight a dual breeding strategy: (i) emphasize additive effects and parental selection for reproductive and yield traits, and (ii) exploit non-additive interactions through hybridization for TYLCV resistance and stress adaptation. This approach aligns with global trends that classify traits according to inheritance patterns (additive or non-additive) and adjust breeding strategies accordingly [ 2 , 44 , 45 ].
This study provides comprehensive insights into the genetic architecture of floral traits and TYLCV resistance in tomato using NCII-based analysis. Additive gene action predominated in traits such as NIMS, NFI, and NFPI, indicating that selection of superior parental lines can effectively enhance reproductive potential. In contrast, TYLCV resistance was largely governed by non-additive effects, emphasizing the need for strategic hybrid development and the exploitation of specific combining ability to achieve durable resistance. Notably, several hybrids (e.g., 323 × 332, 323 × 50, and 73 × 332) combined strong SCA for yield traits with complete viral resistance despite low parental GCA, highlighting the genetic independence of resistance from productivity. This independence provides opportunities for the simultaneous improvement of yield and resilience. Moreover, the lack of significant correlations between GCA and SCA, and between TYLCV resistance and yield traits, reinforces the necessity of integrating both genetic components in hybrid prediction. Collectively, these findings support a dual breeding strategy: leveraging additive effects for reproductive traits while exploiting heterosis and molecular tools such as marker-assisted and genomic selection to develop high-yielding, virus-resistant tomato cultivars.
